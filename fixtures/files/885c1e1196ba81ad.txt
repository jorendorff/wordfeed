										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: More and more, I am hearing people say “not trying to” when they mean “trying not to.” I just saw a woman on YouTube who said, “I wasn&#8217;t trying to take any chances.” What&#8217;s your take on this?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The phrase “not trying to” means lacking the intention or desire to do something, while “trying not to” means actively making an effort to avoid doing something. Both usages are legitimate. Yes, people sometimes use “not trying to” in the sense of “trying not to,” but the meaning is usually clear in context.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">One possible reason for the mixup may be that many Americans pronounce “trying to” as “<em>tryna</em>,” and it may be more natural for them to say “not <em>tryna</em>” than “trying not to,” especially in informal conversation.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">By the way, the You Tuber you cite could have been using “not trying to” in its usual sense if she had been accused of being reckless and responded by saying “I wasn&#8217;t trying to take any chances.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Interestingly, “not trying to” was more common during the 20th century, but “trying not to” has been more popular in the 21st century, according to Google’s <a href="https://books.google.com/ngrams/graph?content=not+trying+to,trying+not+to,trying+to+not&amp;year_start=1900&amp;year_end=2022&amp;corpus=en&amp;smoothing=3">Ngram Viewer</a>, which compares the use of words and phrases in digitized books. As for “trying to not,” it’s a distant third.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A spot check of 21st-century examples suggests people generally use “not trying to” and “trying not to” in the usual way. Here are a couple of examples from book titles: <em>Mother Nature Is Not Trying to Kill You</em> (2002), a wilderness survival guide by Rob Nelson and Haley Chamberlain Nelson, and <em>Trying Not to Love You</em> (2026), a novel by Kaylee Ryan.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the etymology, the English verb “try” is derived from the Old French <em>trier</em> (to pick out or choose). When it first appeared in Middle English in the early 14th century, it meant “to separate (one thing) from another or others,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the earliest <em>OED</em> citation, victorious Britons separate themselves on the battlefield from the defeated Romans: “Þey turnde ageyn, And tryde þe Bretons fro ilk Romeyn” (“They turned back, And tried [separated] the Britons from each Roman”). From Robert Mannyng’s Middle English translation (circa 1330) of <em>Roman de Brut</em>, a 12-century verse history of Britain by the Norman poet Wace.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The separating sense of “try” is now obsolete, but it’s reflected in several modern senses, including the legal use of to “try” someone, which the <em>OED</em> defines as “to determine the guilt or otherwise of (an accused person) by consideration of the evidence.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first citation for this sense is from a Latin-English dictionary: “<em>Interrogari legibus</em>, to be tried by examination, that they had offended against the lawis” (<em>The Dictionary of Syr Thomas Eliot Knyght</em>, 1538).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>OED</em> citation for the sense you’re asking about (“to make an effort, endeavour, attempt”) is from Dryden’s 1697 translation of Virgil&#8217;s <em>Georgics</em> (Book III), a poem about animal husbandry written in the first century BC.</p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In this passage a defeated bull attempts to recover for a second round with the bull that defeated him: “to repair his Strength he tries: Hardning his Limbs with painful Exercise.”</span></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, the participial adjective “trying” in the title of this post (“Trying times”) is derived from the use of the verb “try” to mean “subject to a severe test or strain.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>OED</em> citation for this sense of &#8220;try&#8221; refers to the persecution of Old Testament prophets: “Other were tried w<sup>t</sup> mockynges &amp; scourgynges, moreouer, w<sup>t</sup> bondes &amp; presonment.” From <em>The Byble in Englyshe</em> (1539), Hebrews 11:36.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I’m rereading  Josephine Tey’s <em>Miss Pym Disposes</em> and came across something that has always puzzled me. Teresa suggests to Miss Pym that they get coffee at a teashop called The Teapot. Miss Pym replies, “A little Hibernian, surely?” Can you enlighten me as to what Miss Pym means?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: We think Josephine Tey is using “Hibernian” here to mean “Irish,” which has had the colloquial sense of illogical or paradoxical for several centuries. In <em>Miss Pym Disposes</em>, the paradox is going for coffee at a teashop.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The oldest of these terms is the adjective “Irish,” which was spelled <em>yriscan</em> (roughly UR-iss-kahn) when it appeared in Old English and referred to a native of Ireland. The earliest example in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> is from a passage in the <em>Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</em> for the year 1055:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Hig gegaderadan ða mycle fyrde mid ðam yriscan mannan &amp; mid Walkynne” (“They gathered then a great army with the Irish men and with the Welsh men”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> says a “somewhat offensive” colloquial sense of “Irish” appeared in the early 19th century and referred to “a statement or action” that was “paradoxical; illogical or apparently so.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation, which we’ve expanded, is in a letter dated Feb. 5, 1820, from Henry Peter Brougham, a Whig Member of Parliament, to Thomas Creevey, a Whig politician.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the letter, Brougham responds to a suggestion by Creevey that he send a courier to Queen Caroline by saying he has already sent one, and then apologizing parenthetically for the clumsiness of his wording:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"> “Your advice has been followed by anticipation (to speak Irish); at this moment my courier is within a couple of days’ journey of the Queen.” From the <em>The Creevey Papers</em> (1903), edited by Herbert Maxwell.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the word “Irish” had been used similarly in the noun phrase “Irish bull,” which the <em>OED</em> says appeared nearly a century earlier and referred to “a statement which is manifestly self-contradictory or inconsistent, esp. to humorous effect.” The dictionary notes that this usage is “now likely to be considered offensive.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>Oxford</em> example is from the title of a poem by the Irish poet Mary Barber: “To the Right Honourable the Earl of Thormond, at Bath; who Charg&#8217;d the Author with Making an Irish Bull.” In the poem, she reminds the Earl of his own Irish heritage and says it’s unbecoming of him to mock the speech of his native island.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> says the noun “bull” in “Irish bull” is “of unknown origin,” but it notes that <em>boul</em> in Old French meant fraud, deceit, or trickery, while the noun “bull” in Middle English meant a falsehood and the verb “bull” meant to fool, mock, or cheat.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In early modern English, the dictionary says, the noun “bull” took on the sense “a self-contradictory proposition” and “in modern use, an expression containing a manifest contradiction in terms or involving a ludicrous inconsistency unperceived by the speaker.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>OED</em> citation for the noun used this way is from <em>The Antipodes: a Comedie</em> (1640), by the British playwright Richard Brome: “Dumbe Speaker! that&#8217;s a Bull.” (Brome later puns on “bull” by using it in the bovine sense.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Getting back to your question, when the adjective “Hibernian&#8221; appeared in early modern English, it meant, as it usually means today, “of or belonging to Ireland; Irish,” according to the <em>OED</em>. The dictionary’s first citation is from a book by the Scottish writer William Lithgow about his travels:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The conscionable carriage of the Hybernian Clergy.” From <em>The Totall Discourse, of the Rare Aduentures, and Painefull Peregrinations of Long Nineteene Yeares Trauayles, from Scotland, to the Most Famous Kingdomes in Europe, Asia, and Affrica,</em> (1632).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But in the dictionary’s next citation, Alexander Pope uses “Hibernian” in the sense you’re asking about. Pope uses the adjective in referring to a passage, later corrected, in his poem “An Essay on Criticism”: “ ’Tis right Hibernian, and I confess it what the English call a bull” (in a letter dated June 25, 1711, from Pope to John Caryl).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"> We hope we’ve helped you dispose of your confusion over the use of “Hibernian” in <em>Miss Pym Disposes</em>.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: The Wall Street Journal recently cited speculation that Russia’s aggressive military recruitment “preludes another draft.” If I had written it, I would have used “presages.” Any thoughts?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Yes, we too would have used a verb like “presage” or “foreshadow” instead of “prelude,” which can mean to introduce or precede but lacks the ominous sense of the other words.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But it’s interesting that in the past, “prelude” did sometimes have a sense of foreboding, as did <em>praeludere</em>, the Latin source of the English verb.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In classical Latin, <em>praeludere</em> meant to compose a prelude or make a trial attempt, but in post-classical Latin, it could also mean to introduce or foreshadow, according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When the word “prelude” first appeared in English in the 16th century, the <em>OED</em> says, it was a noun meaning “an introduction, a preface; a precursor.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation is from a 1548 English translation, edited by Nicholas Udall, of Erasmus’s Latin <em>Paraphrases</em> (retellings of the Gospels):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“They shall only be preludes [Latin <em>præludia</em>] of the ende yt is to come” (from the paraphrase of Mark 13, where Jesus predicts the destruction of the temple in Jerusalem and the end of the world).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When the verb “prelude” first appeared in English in the 17th century, the <em>OED</em> says, it meant “to form or act as a prelude, to be introductory to a subsequent work, action, event, etc.” The first <em>Oxford</em> example for the verb uses the gerund “preluding” in an Anglican theological polemic:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The matter of your discourse being of Gods infinite wisedome, and to that purpose preluding of the imperfect wisedom of man” (from <em>Discovery of D.</em><em> Iacksons Vanitie</em>, 1631, by William Twisse). In the polemic, the Calvinist Twisse attacks the writings of the anti-Calvinist Dr. Thomas Jackson.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The verb soon took on the sense of to foreshadow, according to the <em>OED</em>. In the dictionary’s first example, the Welsh poet Henry Vaughan uses it in a metaphor for human mortality:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The Sun now stoops, and hasts his beams to hide Under the dark, and melancholy Earth. All but preludes thy End” (from “Rules and Lessons,” a poem in <em>Silex Scintillans, or Sacred Poems and Private Ejaculations, </em>1650). In Latin, <em>silex scintillans </em>means “sparkling flint.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, standard dictionaries now generally list these senses for the verb “prelude”: (1) to be an introduction or precedent; (2) to introduce or precede, (3) to play a musical prelude. Here’s an example from a report on Fox News about the opening of the recent World Cup competition (Jun. 12, 2026):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“After the numerous opening performances that preluded the start of the 2026 World Cup inside the iconic, colorful and majestic Mexico City Stadium, Mexico finally kickstarted the tournament with a joyous and confident victory.”</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: The word “works” (meaning “factory”) is a hard nut to crack. It looks plural but acts singular. Can it be used to speak about two factories? And how did it come to mean a factory in the first place?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The noun “work” has been used in both singular and plural forms to mean a factory or plant since the 16th century, but the plural &#8220;works&#8221; is the usual form now and can refer to one or more industrial sites.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As the online <em>Merriam-Webster</em> dictionary explains, “works” is “plural in form but singular or plural in construction” when it refers to “a place where industrial labor is carried on.” <em>M-W</em> has these examples: “got a job at a cement/lead works” &#8230; “The local steel works have shut down.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And here’s a recent example we’ve found where the term refers to more than one plant: “Union plans to escalate action over job losses at two steelworks in Wales” (headline in The Guardian, London, June 2, 2024).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The noun was plural when first used to mean “an establishment or premises in which industrial or manufacturing processes are carried out,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> notes that the term is often &#8220;the second element in compounds.” In the dictionary’s earliest example, the compound “iron workes” is used in a statute from the reign of Queen Elizabeth I to protect forests needed to provide wood for shipbuilding.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The citation refers to an exception granted for woods in Surrey that a landowner “preserued and coppised [periodically cut back] for the vse of his Iron workes.” From <em>Act 23 Elizabeth I</em>, known as the Preservation of Wood Act of 1580 (passed in 1581).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest example in the <em>OED</em> for the singular form used in this sense, which we’ve expanded, refers to an Englishman who traveled to France to learn the French method of making glass:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“he return’d, and set up a Glass-work, wherein he far outdid the French, his Teachers.” From the “glass” entry in <em>Cyclopædia; or, an Universal Dictionary of Arts and Sciences</em> (7th edition, 1751), by Ephraim Chambers.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The latest citation in the <em>OED</em> uses the plural form in referring to a single plant: “The process sees sewage arriving at the works for treatment” (from The Independent, London, Oct. 5, 2010).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">If you’d like to read more, we’ve written several posts over the years about other nouns with plural forms that can be used in singular or plural expressions, including “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2015/02/headquarters.html">headquarters</a>,” “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2018/10/whereabouts.html">whereabouts</a>,” and “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/02/whats-the-singular-of-scissors.html">scissors</a>.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in a 2012 post, “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2012/10/clothed-minded.html">Clothed minded</a>,” we discuss why garments with legs (or leg holes) are generally plural, while those with arms (or arm holes) are usually singular.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I have noticed the absence of the subjunctive in lines from recent shows and movies. Have you?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The English subjunctive has been gradually disappearing since Anglo-Saxon days, with ups and downs along the way, but it hasn’t disappeared yet. In fact, it’s quite easy to find recent examples from Broadway to Hollywood. Here are two that use “were” subjunctively to express hypothetical situations.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the current Broadway comedy <em>Oh, Mary!</em>, Abe Lincoln is shocked that his wife wants to become a cabaret star: “How would it look if the First Lady of the United States were to do a cabaret act while the country is at war?!” Her response: “Sensational!”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in the 2026 Hollywood comedy thriller <em>They Will Kill You</em>, the character Asia Reaves delivers this warning line to the leader of a satanic cult: “If I were you, I’d clear out.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In Old English (spoken from roughly 450 to 1150), the subjunctive mood had distinct verb endings. It expressed wishes, requests, possibilities, doubts, and hypothetical situations, as it does in contemporary English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In this example from an Old English version of the Lord’s Prayer in the <em>West Saxon Gospels</em>, the first word (<em>si</em>) is the present subjunctive of the verb <em>beon</em> (to be): “Si þin nama gehalgod” (“Be thine name hallowed”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">English lost its verb endings for the subjunctive, as well as the indicative and imperative moods, during the transitional period from Old English to Middle English—roughly from the 11th to the 13th centuries. In place of the verb endings, the subjunctive was usually expressed with “be,” “were,” and the bare, or  “to”-less, infinitives of other verbs.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In this Middle English example, Chaucer uses the bare infinitive “save” subjunctively in referring to a company of pilgrims: “And God save al this faire compaignye” (from <em>The Knight’s Tale</em> in <em>The Canterbury Tales, </em>circa 1385).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In late Middle English, writers began replacing the subjunctive with expressions using modal auxiliary verbs such as “may,” “might,” “shall,” and “should.” Here’s an example with “should” and “might” from a Jan. 30, 1453, letter by Margaret Paston to her husband, John Paston:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“ye shuld yeve þe jantylman þat ye know of seche langage as he myght fele by yow þat ye wull be wele willyng” (“you should give the gentleman that you know of such language as he might tell by you that you will be well willing”). From <em>Paston Letters and Papers of the Fifteenth Century, Part I</em> (1971).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In early Modern English, Shakespeare used both the subjunctive and modal auxiliaries to express wishes, doubts, possibilities, and so on. He uses the subjunctive “were” as well as the modals “might” and “would” in Act 1, Scene 7 of <em>Macbeth</em>. We’re citing here from the First Folio, 1623:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“If it were done, when ’tis done, then ’twer well, It were done quickly,” Macbeth says, and later adds, “that but this blow Might be the be all, and the end all. Heere,<strong> </strong>But heere, vpon this Banke and Schoole [shoal] of time, Wee’ld iumpe [jump] the life to come.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The use of the subjunctive has continued to decline in Modern English in favor of modal auxiliaries, but it has declined much less<strong> </strong>in the US than in the UK since the late 19th century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">For example, linguists say the use of “were” as the past, or irealis, subjunctive to express hypothetical situations (as in “I wish I were rich”) is much more common in American English than in British English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And linguists have reported an increase in the use of the mandative subjunctive in the US to express requests or demands (as in “She insists that I be on time tomorrow”). That use has apparently influenced a lesser increase in the UK, according to linguists.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Getting back to your question, we haven’t noticed a recent absence or significant decrease in the use of the subjunctive in English.</p>

But I always understood it to be an acronym only when the initials can be pronounced as a word. A: It’s not just journalists. 										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: It seems as if journalists are now calling the initials of every organization an acronym. But I always understood it to be an acronym only when the initials can be pronounced as a word.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: It’s not just journalists. Many English speakers use the term “acronym” to mean an abbreviation with individual letters pronounced. In fact, that’s what “acronym” meant when it first appeared in English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As it turns out, language authorities are divided today on whether an “acronym” is an abbreviation pronounced as one word or as individual letters.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Some standard dictionaries agree with you that an &#8220;acronym&#8221; is an abbreviation formed from the initial letters of the words in a name or phrase and pronounced as a single word (such as “NATO” or “radar”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, others say the term “acronym” can also refer to an initialism—an abbreviation formed from initial letters with each letter pronounced (such as “FBI&#8221; or “CIA”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The online <em>Cambridge Dictionary</em>, for example, takes the narrower view, defining “acronym” as “an abbreviation consisting of the first letters of each word in the name of something, pronounced as a word.” It has this example: “AIDS is an acronym for ‘Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome.’ ”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Merriam-Webster</em> online defines “acronym” similarly but also includes a second sense: “an abbreviation (such as <em>FBI</em>) formed from initial letters: <em>INITIALISM</em>.” In its entry for “initialism,” <em>M-W</em> includes this in a usage note:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Some people feel strongly that <em>acronym</em> should only be used for terms like <em>NATO</em>, which is pronounced as a single word, and that<em> initialism</em> should be used if the individual letters are all pronounced distinctly, as with <em>FBI</em>. You can keep the distinction if you like, but our research shows that <em>acronym</em> is commonly used to refer to both types of abbreviations.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Usage guides generally agree with you that “acronym” should refer to a pronounceable word. For example, <em>Fowler&#8217;s Dictionary of Modern English Usage</em> (4th ed.), edited by Jeremy Butterfield, says an “acronym” is an abbreviation “formed from the initial letters of other words and pronounced as a single word.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the word “acronym” originally referred to an initialism when it first appeared in English in the mid-20th century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference, says it then meant “a group of initial letters used as an abbreviation for a name or expression, each letter or part being pronounced separately; an initialism (such as <em>ATM</em>, <em>TLS</em>).”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation is from <em>Paris Gazette</em> (1940), Willa and Edwin Muir’s English translation of <em>Exil</em>, an anti-Nazi novel in German by Lion Feuchtwanger: “Pee-gee-enn. It’s an acronym [German <em>Akronym</em>], that’s what it is. That&#8217;s what they call words made up of initials.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Later <em>Oxford</em> citations indicate that “acronym” has been used regularly since then in the sense of an initialism. The dictionary’s latest example refers to the former French President Nicolas Sarkozy: “The acronym TSS—<em>Tout Sauf Sarkozy</em> (‘Anything But Sarkozy’).” From “Un Homme in Full,” The Atlantic, June 2008.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So when did “acronym” come to mean an abbreviation pronounced as one word?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A few years after the word first appeared in English in the sense of an initialism, the <em>OED</em> says, English speakers began using it to mean “a word formed from the initial letters of other words or (occasionally) from the initial parts of syllables taken from other words, the whole being pronounced as a single word.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first example for this sense is from “Initials Into Words,” by Basil Davenport (American Notes and Queries, February, 1943). Here’s an expanded version of the citation:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Your correspondent who asks about words made up of the initial letters or syllables of other words may be interested in knowing that I have seen such words called by the name acronym, which is useful and clear to anyone who knows a little Greek.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The term ultimately comes from the Ancient Greek combining forms ἀκρο (<em>akro</em>, tip, peak, or extremity) and ωνυμ (<em>ōnym</em>, name)—hence, a word formed from the tips, or initial letters, of other words.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<div dir="auto">Q: I would love to see a story on “performative gratitude,” a <span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">term for </span><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">expressing gratefulness when you </span><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">don’t really mean it. The usage seems to be flooding society.</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">A: <span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">As far as we can tell, “performative gratitude” first appeared about a dozen years ago on social media as a term for a formulaic or stock expression of thanks.</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">It <span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">then spread to more mainstream media, though the word “performative” had been used earlier in various similar senses.</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">The phrase hasn’t been recognized yet by any of the ten standard dictionaries we regularly consult or by the <i>Oxford English Dictionary</i>, an etymological reference with more than 900,000 entries.</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">The earliest example we’ve seen is from Twitter<span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">: “turn-offs: performative gratitude” (jamie@jmsnftzptrck, Feb. 15, 2014). The same author followed up with another tweet two years later: “ban performative gratitude and feigned modesty from poetry” (May 16, 2016). Several other tweeters then jumped in with comments of their own.</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">Soon the phrase made the leap from social media. An article on HuffPost that appeared just before the holiday season in 2016 refers to “performative gratitude” at the Thanksgiving table:</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">“Previous research suggests that teenagers may not benefit from performative gratitude, such as going around the table to say what you appreciate” (“Why Practicing Gratitude Matters, Even When Everything Is Awful,” by Allison Fox, Nov. 22, 2016).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">And this example is from a research paper on how people use technology collaboratively:</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">“Posting publicly may foster performative gratitude and ego-driven motivations” (from a paper by researchers at the University of California, Santa Cruz, in CSCW ’23 Companion, a publication for an October 2023 conference in Minneapolis on CSCW, or computer-supported cooperative work).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">A year later, the term showed up in The Wall Street Journal: “Performative gratitude—compelling ourselves to be grateful when we’re not—is a form of toxic positivity” (from “The Case for Being Ungrateful,” by Elizabeth Bernstein, a columnist who writes about social psychology, Nov. 24, 2024).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">Finally, here’s an example from earlier this year in Psychology Today: “<i>Performative gratitude</i> occurs when we express thanks not out of genuine appreciation but to burnish our social image” (“Rogue Gratitude: When Thankfulness Becomes a Vice,” by the psychologist Joel Wong, April 22, 2026).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">As for the etymology here, we’ll start with the much earlier “gratitude,” a noun borrowed from French in the mid-16th century and ultimately traceable to the Latin <i>gratus</i> (pleasing, thankful).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">In the <i>OED</i>’s earliest example, from a Latin-English dictionary, the word is used to mean “the quality or condition of being grateful”—the sense it has today. Here’s the dictionary’s citation:</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto">&#8220;<i>Gratitudo</i>, Kindnes: gratitude: thankefulnes” (<i>Thesaurus Linguæ Romanæ &amp; Britannicæ</i>, 1565, by the English theologian and bishop Thomas Cooper. In the 16th century, the term “thesaurus” referred to a dictionary, encyclopedia, or the like. It didn’t come to mean a collection of synonyms until the mid-19th century).</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
</div>
<div dir="auto">When the adjective “performative” first appeared in the early 20th century, according to the <i>OED, </i>it referred to “an utterance that effects an action by being spoken or by means of which the speaker performs a particular act.” An example would be a justice of the peace saying, “I now pronounce you husband and wife.”</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto"><i>Oxford</i>’s first citation for this sense is from “Memory: A Triphase Objective Action,” a study by J. R. Kantor in the Journal of Philosophy, Nov. 9, 1922.</div>
</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto">Here we’ll cite what we consider a clearer example from the same article. In distinguishing between “informational and performative memory acts,” Kantor defines the latter as “memory acts which constitute some actual work to be done.&#8221;</div>
</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto">Later in the century, “performative” took on what the <i>OED</i> describes as a “usually disparaging” sense of  “speech, behaviour, etc.: done or expressed for the sake of appearance, esp. to impress others or to improve one&#8217;s own image (typically with the implication of insincere intent or superficial impact).”</div>
</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto"><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">The dictionary’s first citation is from </span><i style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">The Erotics of Talk: Women’s Writing and Feminist Paradigms</i><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;"> (1996), by Carla Kaplan: “If we are to provide more than merely performative or poetic justice we need clearer blueprints for meaningful social action.”</span></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">This disparaging sense of “performative” is how the word is used in the phrase “performative gratitude.” More neutral phrases for the same notion are “ritual politeness,” “formulaic courtesy,” “social nicety,” “conventional courtesy,” “perfunctory gratitude,” “pro-forma thanks,” and so on.</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
</div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto">A linguist might use the term “phatic expression” to describe &#8220;thank you&#8221; and other speech used for social purposes rather than for communicating information.</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
</div>
<div dir="auto">The adjective “phatic” appeared in English in the early 20th century and comes from the ancient Greek φατός (<i>phatos</i>, “spoken”), an ancestor of “euphemism.”</div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto"><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">The <em>OED</em></span><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;"> defines “phatic” here as describing “speech, utterances, etc., that serve to establish or maintain social relationships rather than to impart information, communicate ideas, etc.”</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">The dictionary notes its use <span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em;">in the phrase “phatic communion,” meaning “speech communication of this kind; (also) trivial or purely formal verbal contact.&#8221;</span></div>
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto">The dictionary&#8217;s first citation <span style="text-align: right; letter-spacing: -0.015em;">is from “The Problem of Meaning in Primitive Languages,” </span><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em; text-align: right;">a contribution by the anthropologist and ethnologist Bronisław Malinowski to <i>Meaning of Meaning </i>(1923), a book by C. K. Ogden and A. Richards:</span></div>
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto">
<div dir="auto"></div>
<div dir="auto"><span style="letter-spacing: -0.015em; text-align: right;">“There can be no doubt that we have here a new type of linguistic use—phatic communion I am tempted to call it, &#8230; a type of speech in which ties of union are created by a mere exchange of words.” </span></div>

And why are “see” and “hear” now dynamic, not stative, on TV news? Grammar books say you can’t use progressive tenses with stative verbs, but I often hear reporters say things like &#8220;We are seeing a [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/06/see-hear.html">On ‘seeing’  and ‘hearing’</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Why is the present tense, not the past, used in this sentence: “I hear you&#8217;re leaving the country”? And why are “see” and “hear” now dynamic, not stative, on TV news? Grammar books say you can’t use progressive tenses with stative verbs, but I often hear reporters say things like &#8220;We are seeing a forest in flames.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The verbs “see” and “hear” can express either states or actions. As stative verbs, one “sees” (perceives with the eyes) a rainbow or “hears” (perceives with the ears) thunder. As action, or dynamic, verbs, “see” and “hear” have many other senses:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“See” can mean <u>to be a spectator</u> (“I’m seeing Hamlet next week”); <u>to understand</u> (“He could see what Rhonda meant”); <u>to experience</u> <u>(</u>“I don’t want to see another hurricane”); <u>to meet</u> (“The doctor is seeing patients now”); <u>to visit</u> (“I’m seeing the Eiffel Tower tomorrow”), and so on.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Hear” can mean <u>to take up a legal issue</u> (“The judge is hearing evidence next week”); <u>to pay attention</u> (“He never hears what I say”): <u>to be informed</u> (“Did you hear what Billie bought?”; <u>to attend</u> (“Are you hearing the <em>Mass in B Minor</em> tomorrow?”); <u>to detect</u> (“I could hear anger in her voice”), and so on.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As you can see by the examples above, the active senses of the verbs “see” and “hear” are often used in progressive (“-ing”) tenses. The usage isn’t all that new. Here’s an example we’ve found from the mid-19th century:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“ ‘I travel for improvement. I’m seeing the world’— said Miss Judd, pertinaciously walking out of the room” (from “Nothing Morally Wrong,” a short story by Eliza Leslie in Godey’s Magazine and Lady’s Book, Philadelphia, July 1849).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And here’s a nonfiction example from the later 19th century: “I’m seeing the old man just now about fixing up three of my machines” (from <em>A Fast Life on the Modern Highway</em>, 1874, by Joseph Taylor, a book about the railroad industry).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ve already written about the use of the present tense by authors and journalists to express the past and the future. In a 2013 post, <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2013/01/literary-present.html">When the past is present</a>, we discuss the use of the present to quote someone else’s words, a convention sometimes referred to as the “literary present.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In literary criticism and other writing about authors, for example, you’ll see comments like “As Tacitus says …” or “In his earlier novels, Roth writes …” or “Here’s how Alan Furst describes ….”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In a 2009 post, <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2009/02/tense-about-the-present.html">Tense about the present,</a> we discuss  a similar convention, often called the “historical present,” where the present tense is used to narrate events in the past. This device is used frequently in nonfiction writing and journalism.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We invented an example: “The walls of Troy are still standing after a 10-year siege by the Greeks. Then Odysseus and his best warriors hide inside a giant wooden horse and trick the Trojans into dragging it inside the gates. At night, the Greeks slip out and let in the rest of their army.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Why the present tense? As we explain in our earlier posts, because a literary work—no matter when it was created—presents itself to the reader in an unchanging present, as if it were being written as we read.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, when referring to a literary work in relation to its historical time, the past tense is generally used: “Shakespeare wrote <em>The Tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark</em> in the late 16th or early 17th centuries.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Newspaper headline writers also use the present tense for the past or future, as in this headline in The New York Times on July 21, 1969: “MEN WALK ON MOON.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And broadcasters often use the present in headline-ese teasers: “Mom dies in leap from bridge–news at eleven!” In covering sports on the air, the present may be used for the past or future in play-by-play commentary: “If he catches that pass, he scores easily.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ll end with an example of “see” and “hear” from the The Gospel of Matthew (13:13) in the West Saxon Gospels, dating from the Old English of the late 10th century:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“For-þam ic spece to heom mid byspellen. for-þam þe lokiende hyo ne geseoð. &amp; ge-herende hyo ne ge-hereð. ne ne on-geteð” (“Therefore I speak to them with parables: because looking they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor do they understand).&#8221;</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I was discussing well-being this morning and wondered about its antonym. AI says “the opposite of wellbeing is often considered to be illbeing, which refers to a state of poor health or unhappiness.” Do you have any thoughts about this?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: You can find &#8220;ill-being&#8221; in several standard dictionaries. <em>Merriam-Webster</em>, for example, defines it as “a condition of being deficient in health, happiness, or prosperity,” and <em>American Heritage</em> as “lack of prosperity, happiness, or health.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference, says it refers to an “ ‘ill’ or unprosperous condition; employed as the antithesis of <em>well-being</em>.” Both terms are usually hyphenated now.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> indicates that “ill-being” is relatively rare, with “about 0.02 occurrences per million words in modern written English.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the more common “well-being,” <em>Oxford</em> defines it as “the state of being healthy, happy, or prosperous; physical, psychological, or moral welfare.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A search for “ill-being” and “well-being” in Google’s <a href="https://books.google.com/ngrams/graph?content=ill-being,+well-being&amp;year_start=1800&amp;year_end=2022&amp;corpus=en&amp;smoothing=3">Ngram viewer</a>, which compares words and phrases in digitized books, confirms the rarity of “ill-being.” In fact, the <em>OED</em> has only two examples of the usage, both from the 19th century:</p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“The test of vital well-being or illbeing to a generation.” From <em>On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History </em>(1841), by Thomas Carlyle. (In later edited editions, both terms are either hyphenated or unhyphenated.)</li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“Philanthropists … insuring the future ill-being of men while eagerly pursuing their present well-being.” From Herbert Spencer’s <em>The Man Versus the State </em>(1884), a work of political theory combining four articles published earlier in the year in the Contemporary Review, a British journal.</li>
</ul>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Interestingly, we found these much older, archaic senses of “well-being” and “ill-being” while looking into your question: “being blessed by God” and “being damned by God.” Both senses appear in this passage from <em>Fifty Sermons, Preached by That Learned and Reverend Divine, John Donne</em> (1649):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Our <em>Esse</em>, our <em>Being</em>, is from Gods saying, <em>Dixit</em> &amp; <em>facti</em>, God spoke, and we were made: our <em>Bene esse</em>, <em>our well-being</em>, is from Gods saying too; <em>Bene-dicit</em> God blesses us, in speaking gratiously to us. Even our <em>ill-being</em>, our condemnation is from Gods saying also: for <em>Malediction</em> is <em>Damnation</em>. So far God hath gone with us that way, as that our Being, our well-being, our ill-being is from his saying.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> doesn’t include those two archaic meanings of “well-being” and “ill-being,” but it does cite several obsolete or archaic uses of “ill” and &#8220;well&#8221; as applied to persons of either evil or good character.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Although &#8220;ill-being” is relatively rare now, it does appear once in a while, as in this title of a recent research article: “The nature of the relation between mental well-being and ill-being” (Nature Human Behaviour, December 2025).</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Where or when did the phrase “cutting corners” show up?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: When the usage first appeared in writing in the early 19th century, it had to do with riders and drivers who made their horses take corners and bends in the road too sharply. And as far as we know, it was first used in the United States.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest example we’ve found is in a letter to the editor printed in a Baltimore monthly, the American Turf Register and Sporting Magazine, August 1831. Here the correspondent, signing himself “Tandem” (a carriage term), brags about the excellence of Americans in “riding and driving”:</p>
<p>&#8220;We have young gentlemen in Baltimore, of taste and fortune, who can handle the ribbands [reins] as well, and cut a corner as close, as any whip [coachman] in old England.”</p>
<p>However, we&#8217;ve also found the notion of cutting a corner used in boating (1840s) and in fox hunting (1850s).  Here&#8217;s a boating example:</p>
<p>&#8220;Our present accident was in consequence of the boatmen endeavouring to cut a corner off, and to get before one of the other boats, which had started first.&#8221; From <em>Five Years in the East</em>, Vol. II (London, 1847), by R. N. Hutton, the pseudonym of Charles Henry Newmarch.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And this fox-hunting example refers to riders who cut away from the hounds and ride toward where a fox appears to be heading instead of staying with the pack following the fox’s meandering trail. The passage includes both “cutting corners” and “cross riding” (crossing the path of another rider):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“About a hundred and fifty horsemen were at once scattered over the downs, riding at the top of their speed, in almost all directions; some following the hounds, but a greater number, not liking the undulating nature of the ground, cutting corners, and hustling each other by cross riding.” From <em>Letters on the Management of Hounds</em> (1852), by William Horlock Knightley.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>’s earliest citation, which is from later in the 1850s, to “cut corners” means “to pass round (a corner) as close to the inside of the bend as possible.” The dictionary says it usually implies careless or dangerous driving, as in this passage:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The groom sent down for the carriage gets drunk, drives full gallop up the Pass, and, cutting a corner rather too fine, goes over the precipice.” From a review of Samuel White Baker&#8217;s book <em>Eight Years&#8217; Wanderings in Ceylon </em>(1855), published in the New Quarterly Review in 1856.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> says “<em>to cut corners</em> and variants” consequently came to mean “to pursue a course of action that saves time or makes things easy, but is hazardous or unorthodox.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Oxford</em>’s earliest citation for this sense is from a newspaper in Yorkshire, England: “The art of ‘cutting the corners,’ and other less legitimate tricks” (Huddersfield Examiner, July 25, 1868).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary says the expression now usually means “to do something in a perfunctory or inattentive way (often omitting a step or component or substituting something inferior), typically so as to reduce the expense associated with the activity. Sometimes also: to act illegally or untruthfully.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the latest <em>OED</em> citation, corners are cut to reduce prices: “To compete, some also promise more than they can deliver while others cut corners to offer low prices” (from The Straits Times, an English language newspaper in Singapore, Aug. 23, 2013).</p>

		<title>‘My’ and ‘mine’ shadow</title>
		<link>https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/05/my-mine.html?utm_source=rss&#038;utm_medium=rss&#038;utm_campaign=my-mine</link>
		
		<dc:creator><![CDATA[Pat and Stewart]]></dc:creator>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>‘Q: I am used to reading older texts that use “my” before consonants (“my love”) and “mine” before vowels (“mine eyes”). But once in a while I see them used the same way. In the King James Version, Psalm 119 has “my affliction” (verse 50) and “mine affliction” (verses 92 and 153). Were “my” and [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/05/my-mine.html">‘My’ and ‘mine’ shadow</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">‘Q: I am used to reading older texts that use “my” before consonants (“my love”) and “mine” before vowels (“mine eyes”). But once in a while I see them used the same way. In the <em>King James Version, </em>Psalm 119 has “my affliction” (verse 50) and “mine affliction” (verses 92 and 153). Were “my” and “mine” somewhat interchangeable at the discretion of the writer?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: When “mine” first appeared in Old English as a singular possessive adjective (spelled <em>min</em>, <em>myn</em>, <em>mine</em>, etc.), it was used before nouns beginning with consonants as well as vowels, but it gradually came to be used only before nouns with an initial vowel or consonant “h.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When “my,” a shortened version of “mine,” began appearing in Middle English (as <em>mie</em>, <em>mi</em>, <em>my</em>, etc.), it was used before all consonants except “h,” but it evolved over the years to become the universal possessive adjective corresponding to the pronoun “I” in Modern English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the evolution of “my” and “mine” as possessive adjectives was messy in Middle English and early Modern English, and that’s why you’re seeing so many inconsistent examples in your reading.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Let’s look now at the O<em>xford English Dictionary</em>’s two earliest Old English examples of “mine” used as a first-person possessive adjective. The earliest comes before a vowel (<em>mine æ</em>, “mine law”) and the second before a consonant (<em>mines weddes,</em> “of mine pledge”).</p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“Hi hæfdon mine æ, &amp; hi me ne gecnewon” (“They had mine law, and they did not know me”). From King Alfred’s late ninth-century translation of <em>Liber Regulae Pastoralis </em>(“Pastoral Care”), a sixth-century Latin treatise by Pope Gregory.</li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“Þonne beo ic gemyndig mines weddes” (“Then I will be mindful of mine pledge”). From the Benedictine Abbot Ælfric of Eynsham’s <em>Catholic Homilies</em>, written in the late 10th century. (The term <em>mines </em>here is the genitive singular of <em>mine </em>or <em>min</em>, Old English forms of “mine.”)</li>
</ul>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In case you’re wondering, “mine” could appear before “h” whether the consonant was sounded or silent.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here is an Old English example we’ve found in which “mine” comes before a noun beginning with an “h” that’s sounded: “Beo min heorte ⁊ min lichama þurh god ungeƿemmed” (“May mine heart and mine body through God be undefiled”). From Aelfric’s <em>Lives of the Saints</em>, late 10th century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And we’ve found this Middle English example of “mine” before a noun beginning with an “h” that’s silent: “Have ye so greet envye / Of myn honour, that thus [ye] compleyne and crye?” From &#8220;The Knight&#8217;s Tale” in Chaucer’s <em>Canterbury Tales</em>, circa 1385.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for “my,” the <em>OED</em> describes it as a “variant of <em>mine</em> adj. with loss of final ‑<em>n</em>, used originally before consonants except <em>h</em>, which ultimately became the universal possessive adjective of the 1st person singular except in archaic or poetic use.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary notes some isolated examples of “an apparent reduced form” in Old English, but adds that these early “my” sightings &#8220;are perhaps the result of scribal carelessness.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s a questionable example from the <em>West Saxon Gospels</em>, dating from the Old English of the late 10th century: “Se hælend him andswarode &amp; cwæþ: mi lar nis na min, ac þæs þe me sende” (“The Savior answered them and said: My teaching [<em>mi lar</em>] is not mine, but of him who sent me”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary notes that “such reduced forms first occur sporadically in early Middle English before consonants except <em>h</em>, in which use they become general in northern varieties of Middle English by the end of the 13th cent.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Oxford</em> adds that the use of “my” before consonants spread to “other areas during the course of the 14th and 15th centuries, although occasional examples of <em>mine</em> before consonants are found even in the 16th cent.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And until the end of the 16th century, the <em>OED</em> says, “<em>my</em> often resulted also from the transference of the <em>n</em> of <em>mine</em> to the following noun or adjective by metanalysis,” the process by which the division between words is changed, resulting in a new word (as in “a napron” becoming “an apron”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary notes the appearance of “myne errande,” “myn eronde,” and “mi nerrand” in various manuscripts of <em>Cursor Mundi</em>, an anonymous Middle English poem that dates from the early 1300s. Here’s a passage with “mi nerrand,” a result of metanalysis:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Be þis well sal i habide Quat o mi nerrand” (“By this well I shall abide to see what may become of my errand”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ll end with an early Modern English example from Shakespeare’s 1595 play <em>Richard II</em> (Act 4, Scene 1), in which the deposed king uses the possessive adjectives “my” and “mine” repeatedly in handing over his crown to Bolingbroke (the future Henry IV):</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Now, mark me how I will undo myself.<br />
I give this heavy weight from off my head<br />
And this unwieldy scepter from my hand,<br />
The pride of kingly sway from out my heart.<br />
With mine own tears I wash away my balm,<br />
With mine own hands I give away my crown,<br />
With mine own tongue deny my sacred state,<br />
With mine own breath release all duteous oaths.<br />
All pomp and majesty I do forswear.<br />
My manors, rents, revenues I forgo;<br />
My acts, decrees, and statutes I deny.<br />
God pardon all oaths that are broke to me.<br />
God keep all vows unbroke are made to thee.<br />
Make me, that nothing have, with nothing grieved,<br />
And thou with all pleased that hast all achieved.<br />
Long mayst thou live in Richard’s seat to sit,<br />
And soon lie Richard in an earthy pit.<br />
God save King Henry, unkinged Richard says,<br />
And send him many years of sunshine days.</p>
</blockquote>

Can you shed light on these  opposite meanings? A: Both meanings of “table” ultimately come from tabula, classical Latin for a board used to write on, play on, hold sacred offerings, and so on. [&#8230;]</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: News articles often say an issue is “on the table,” meaning being considered. But “tabling” the issue means putting it off. Can you shed light on these  opposite meanings?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Both meanings of “table” ultimately come from<em> tabula</em>, classical Latin for a board used to write on, play on, hold sacred offerings, and so on.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When the word “table” first appeared in Old English as the noun <em>tabul</em>, the term referred to a tablet used for votive offerings.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first citation in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> is from an Old English translation (circa 900) of <em>Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum</em> (“Ecclesiastical History of the English People”), an eighth-century Latin church history by the Anglo-Saxon monk Bede:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Hæfdan hio mid him gehalgude fatu, &amp; gehalgadne tabul” (“They had with them consecrated vessels and a consecrated tablet”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The noun “table” developed many other meanings in Middle English, including a piece of furniture to work or eat at, but we’ll  skip ahead to the early Modern English of the 16th century when it took on a sense in British politics that the <em>OED</em> defines this way:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The table which stands before the Speaker&#8217;s chair in the House of Commons, on which the mace lies when the Speaker is in the chair, and at which the Clerk of the House and his or her assistants receive motions, questions to ministers, etc., and new members are sworn in. Hence: a similar table in the House of Lords or in other legislatures modelled on the British Parliament.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>Oxford</em> citation for this sense is from a book on parliamentary procedure: “There is onely one Clark belonging to this house, his office is to sit next before the Speaker, at a Table vpon which he writeth and layeth his books” (<em>The Order and Vsage of Keeping of the Parlements in England</em>, c. 1572, by John Hooker).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary says that meaning of the noun “table” led to the use of the verb “table” in the sense of “to lay on the table of a legislative assembly or other deliberative meeting. Now frequently in extended use.” The term means “to present or submit formally for discussion or consideration” in the <em>OED</em>’s earliest example:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Upon the presenting of a petition thereanent [concerning this], whereof at least a hundred, at several times were tabled: your wisdome remedied the plaintiff&#8221; (from <em>Logopandecteision, or An Introduction to the Universal Language</em>, Thomas Urquhart’s 1653 parody about an artificial language).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Oxford</em> says this sense of the verb “table” is “common in English-speaking regions outside the United States.” But in the mid-19th century, the dictionary says, a new sense of the verb originated in the US and is chiefly seen in American English: “to postpone consideration of, esp. indefinitely; to shelve.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>OED</em> example of the American usage is from 1849, published in <em>Whig Almanac 1850</em>: “Senator Westcott tried to table the bill, but failed: it became law.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary doesn’t discuss why the American usage evolved. We think it may have developed from the idea of tabling something for future rather than present consideration.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As it turns out, the expression “on the table” has the same sense in both American and British English: “under consideration or discussion.” We’ll end with the first<em> OED</em> example for the expression:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;The facts are, so to speak, all upon the table, and I will merely touch upon the main heads of my case&#8221; (from <em>Dawn</em>, 1884, the first novel by the British writer H. Rider Haggard).</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I recently encountered a sentence about the need for “state and local leadership on immigration.” This use of “on” strikes me as lazy and inconsiderate of syntax. It’s probably permissible in conversation, but not in edited writing. Or am I wrong?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The preposition “on” has been used by respected writers since Anglo-Saxon days to mean about or concerning, and the usage is recognized by all eight standard dictionaries we regularly consult.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference, defines this sense of “on” as “with reference to, as regards; concerning, about.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s an <em>OED</em> citation from the <em>Old English Boethius</em>, a late ninth- or early tenth-century translation of <em>De Consolatione Philosophiae</em> (“The Consolation of Philosophy”), a Latin treatise by the Roman philosopher Boethius:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Hwæt godes magon we seggan on þa flæsclican unþeawas” (“What good may we say on the fleshly vices?”). The preface in one of two early manuscripts of the work identifies King Alfred as the author, but that attribution is now considered doubtful. The author was likely one of the scholars in his court, though Alfred may have overseen the translation.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Another <em>Oxford</em> example of the usage comes from Chaucer’s Middle English poem <em>Troilus and Criseyde</em>, composed around 1385: “He moste assente on that conclusioun, As for the tyme” (“He must agree on that conclusion, at least for the time being”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">An <em>OED</em> citation from Sir Thomas Malory’s <em>Le Morte Darthur</em>, a late 15th-century Middle English version of the Arthurian legends, says the Knights of the Round Table surpassed all others at jousts and tournaments, “and that was well proved … on [as regards] sir Launcelot de Lake.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Since then, the preposition “on” has been used routinely for “about,” “concerning,” and so on in both formal and informal English. Here are some entries from online standard dictionaries and their examples for the usage:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>American Heritage</em>: “concerning; about: <em>a book on astronomy”</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Cambridge</em>: “about or having something as a subject: <em>Did you see that documentary on volcanoes last night?</em>”; <em>Sarita’s thesis is on George Crumb”</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Collins</em>: “concerned with or relating to: a <em>tax on potatoes; a programme on archaeology”</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Longman</em>: “about a particular subject: <em>an international conference on global warming</em>”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Merriam-Webster</em>: “used as a function word to indicate the subject of study, discussion, or consideration: <em>a book on insects; reflect on that a moment; agree on price</em>”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ll end with an example (for “meditating on&#8221;) from one of our favorite scenes in one of our favorite novels, Jane Austen’s <em>Pride and Prejudice </em>(1813). Here, Darcy is looking contemplative at a party at the home of Sir William Lucas when he’s approached by Miss Bingley:</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“I can guess the subject of your reverie.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“I should imagine not.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“You are considering how insupportable it would be to pass many evenings in this manner,—in such society; and, indeed, I am quite of your opinion. I was never more annoyed! The insipidity, and yet the noise—the nothingness, and yet the self-importance, of all these people! What would I give to hear your strictures on them!”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Your conjecture is totally wrong, I assure you. My mind was more agreeably engaged. I have been meditating on the very great pleasure which a pair of fine eyes in the face of a pretty woman can bestow.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Miss Bingley immediately fixed her eyes on his face, and desired he would tell her what lady had the credit of inspiring such reflections. Mr. Darcy replied, with great intrepidity,—</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Miss Elizabeth Bennet.”</p>
</blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Why a “meteoric rise”? Meteors crash down on Earth.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The use of “meteoric” for something that rises may seem counterintuitive, but the adjective has been used that way for more than a century and a half, and the usage is standard English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, the usage ultimately comes from ancient Greek, where μετέωρος (<em>meteoros</em>), the source of “meteor” and “meteoric,” means raised or aloft.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Standard dictionaries define “meteoric” as relating to meteors or resembling them in speed or brilliance. All eight standard dictionaries we regularly consult use the phrase “meteoric rise” in their examples for the “speedy” sense.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here are some examples: “a meteoric rise to fame” (<em>Merriam-Webster</em>, <em>American Heritage</em>): “His meteoric rise to stardom” (<em>Webster&#8217;s New World</em>); “her meteoric rise from dancer to professional actress” (<em>Longman</em>); “his meteoric rise in politics” (<em>Dictionary.com</em>), and “The group had a meteoric rise to fame in the 1990s” (<em>Cambridge</em>).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When “meteoric” first appeared in the early 17th century, it meant “relating to, or consisting of elements of, both the earth and the heavens,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>. The only <em>OED</em> citation for this obsolete sense is from a letter by John Donne to a friend, Sir Henry Goodyer, believed written in 1612:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Our nature is Meteorique, we respect (because we partake so) both earth and heaven” (from <em>Letters to Severall Persons of Honour Written by John Donne, Sometime Deane of St Pauls, </em>1651).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the late 18th century, the dictionary says, “meteoric” started being used in referring to meteors or meteorites. It cites a satirical column in the Pennsylvania Gazette, Jan. 26, 1785, that compares current events to “a fiery ball” in the sky, then asks readers to pardon “this meteoric digression.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the late 18th or early 19th century, according to <em>OED</em> citations, “meteoric” began being used figuratively to mean “flashing or dazzling like a meteor, transiently or irregularly brilliant; rapid, swift; appearing suddenly.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>Oxford</em> citation for the flashing sense is from “Cheerfulness,” an ode by the Irish poet Thomas Dermody, written sometime before his death in 1802: “His godlike hair, of braided rays, / His vest, a meteoric blaze” (from <em>The Harp of Erin, Containing the Poetical Works of the Late T. Dermody</em>, Vol. II, 1807).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation for the speedy sense is from the Daily Chronicle (London), Jan. 16, 1895: “We had occasion to undertake a somewhat meteoric flight from Balmoral.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, we&#8217;ve found a much earlier example that includes the phrase “meteoric rise.” It compares the suddenness  of Byron’s success as a poet to the “meteoric rise” of Napoleon:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“He called himself, in one of his poems, ‘the grand Napoleon of the realms of rhyme;’ and there is some similarity between the suddenness and splendour of his literary career and the meteoric rise and domination of the First Buonaparte” (from <em>The Student’s Manual of English Literature: A History of English Literature</em>, 1864, by Thomas B. Shaw).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The phrase “meteoric rise” is much more common than “meteoric fall,” according to Google’s <a href="https://books.google.com/ngrams/graph?content=meteoric+rise%2Cmeteoric+fall&amp;year_start=1800&amp;year_end=2022&amp;corpus=en&amp;smoothing=3">Ngram viewer</a>, but the falling usage does appear once in a while, as in this example about the decline of the moth Alabama argillacea:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“<em>Ode to Alabama</em>: The Meteoric Fall of a Once Extraordinarily Abundant Moth” (the headline on an article by David L. Wagner in American Entomologist, July 1, 2009).</p>

		<title>Making the cut</title>
		<link>https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/04/make-the-cut.html?utm_source=rss&#038;utm_medium=rss&#038;utm_campaign=make-the-cut</link>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>Q: &#8220;Making the cut&#8221; is said to originate from golf, but it might equally be said to have its roots in early moviemaking. Which came first? A: The expression “make the cut” didn’t originate in either golf or filmmaking. When it first appeared in print, the expression referred to people who didn’t “make the cut” [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/04/make-the-cut.html">Making the cut</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: &#8220;Making the cut&#8221; is said to originate from golf, but it might equally be said to have its roots in early moviemaking. Which came first?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The expression “make the cut” didn’t originate in either golf or filmmaking. When it first appeared in print, the expression referred to people who didn’t “make the cut” for Christmas bonuses.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> defines the usage as “to succeed in being included in, or admitted to, something; to be good enough for something.” The earliest <em>OED</em> example compares holiday bonuses to profit sharing:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“It wasn&#8217;t really profit sharing, I realized, because it didn&#8217;t include the publisher&#8217;s telephone operator and my own cook. In short, the common man, as usual, didn&#8217;t make the cut” (from “Control,” an essay by E. B. White in Harper&#8217;s New Monthly Magazine, February 1943).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The golf usage appeared a dozen years later. The <em>OED</em> defines it as “to qualify for the last two rounds of a four-round golf tournament, by equaling or bettering the required score.”  The first citation is from a northern California newspaper:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“One local professional did manage to better the 36-hole total of 148 which was necessary to make the cut and qualify for Sunday&#8217;s final round over the Pebble Beach course” (Oakland Tribune, Jan. 16, 1955).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> says a much earlier use of “cut” in golf referred to “spin imparted on the ball which makes it curve in flight towards the right (or for a left-handed player, the left), esp. to a moderate and controlled degree.” It adds that such spin typically “results in the ball stopping quickly rather than rolling on, esp. in shots to the green.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the dictionary’s first citation, the ball stops suddenly on the green: “Almost every professional gets his ball to stop comparatively dead … by means of putting cut upon it” (from <em>Golf</em>, 1890, by Horace G. Hutchinson). The book was part of The Badminton Library of Sports and Pastimes, founded by Henry Somerset, 8th Duke of Beaufort. Badminton was his main country house and the source of the name for the racket sport played with a shuttlecock.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> doesn’t have any filmmaking examples of “make the cut,” though we’ve seen it used literally for making “cuts,” or transitions, in a film. A recent example appears in the title of a book on film editing: <em>Make the Cut: A Guide to Becoming a Successful Assistant Editor in Film and TV</em> (2017), by Lori Coleman and Diana Friedberg.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The noun “cut” has been used since the early 20th century to mean “an immediate transition from one shot or scene to the next”—at first in filmmaking and later in television, video games, social media, etc., according to the <em>OED</em>. The dictionary’s first example is from an early guide to film production:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“There is a cut to some other scene and we come back to Smith standing over Brown with a smoking revolver in his hand.” From <em>The Technique of the Photoplay </em>(2nd ed., 1913), by Epes Winthrop Sargent.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The verb “cut” soon came to be used as a command by a director to stop filming. The earliest <em>Oxford</em> citation is from a California newspaper: “Director Smith yelled ‘cut’ ” (Santa Cruz Evening News, Dec. 22, 1915).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for its early etymology, “cut” is “of uncertain origin,” the <em>OED</em> says, though it was perhaps inherited from prehistoric Germanic or early Scandinavian.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When it first appeared in the late 12th century, “cut” meant “to separate or remove (something) from a main body or larger whole with a knife, axe, or other sharp-edged implement; to lop off.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest example is from <em>Layamon’s Brut</em>, a legendary chronicle of Britain composed sometime before 1200. “He cutte his owe þeh … þar-of he makede breade” (“He cut his own thigh &#8230; thereof he maketh roast meat”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the tale, King Cadwallon and his men are stranded on Guernsey after a shipwreck. The King is starving and says he has a craving for venison. With no deer on the island, his nephew Brian cuts off and roasts a piece of his own flesh. The citation combines passages from two surviving <em>Layamon</em> manuscripts, Caligula (late 13th century) and Otho (early 14th century).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary also cites a manuscript, written in legal Latin around the same time, in which the verb appears as part of the compound “cutpurse,” a nickname for someone who steals purses by cutting the straps holding them to belts or waistbands:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Willelmus Cuttepurs qui occidit Willelmum mercatorem de Corbrigg’ postea captus fuit et coram Justic’ ad gaol’ deliberand’ assign’ suspensus fuit. nulla habuit catall’ ” (“William Cutpurse, who killed William the merchant of Corbridge, was later captured and, before the Justices assigned to deliver him to jail, was hanged. He had no chattels”). From a 1292 entry in the <em>Eyre Roll Cumberland</em> (records of traveling courts).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ll end with a comment from a former filmmaker about a 2022 post in which we discuss the expression “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2022/01/cut-to-the-chase.html">cut to the chase</a>&#8220;:</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As someone who used to inhabit cutting rooms, I think there’s another little element to this one. Why ‘cut’? That’s because in the earlier days of filmmaking, in order to edit a film you literally ‘cut’ the piece you wanted out of the main roll with scissors, and then glued those selected scenes together.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Later, ‘splicers’ turned up―clever little guillotine devices that made far more accurate and consistent cuts to be made, and joins to be made with clear specialist tape to create the ‘cutting copy,’ the first edited version of the film.</p>
</blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Over the last decade I&#8217;ve been seeing an uptick in the use of “drop” to mean something new being released, like a podcast episode or music album. Where does this come from?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Something must be in the air. Two people have asked us this same question within a week.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The use of “drop” as a verb or noun for the release of new music, software, movies, podcasts, and so on apparently originated in the late 1980s in the world of hip-hop and rap.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Several online standard dictionaries now have entries for “drop” used this way in speech and informal writing.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Merriam-Webster</em>, for example, defines the noun as “something (such as a song) that is released to the public.” And <em>Cambridge</em> says the verb means “to become available for people to buy, listen to, or watch, especially using the internet.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The two earliest examples in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference,<em> </em>are from an interview with Joseph Simmons of the hip-hop trio Run-DMC in the May 1988 issue of the music magazine Spin.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Simmons uses the verb both transitively (with an object) and intransitively (without one) in these two citations: (1) “I think that I should be able to drop records when I want.”  (2) “Maybe after my album drops and I’m back on the road doing what I’m supposed to do in this world, I&#8217;ll be happy.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> cites a somewhat similar use of “drop” that appeared a few months later: “to sing or perform rap lyrics or rap music.&#8221; The dictionary’s first citation, which we’ve expanded, is from an Oct. 10, 1988, article in The Los Angles Times about a performance by the hip-hop duo DJ Jazzy Jeff &amp; the Fresh Prince:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;The Fresh Prince gave himself and Jazzy Jeff a last-gasp pep talk before performing their current hit, ‘A Nightmare on My Street,’ a parody of the ‘Nightmare on Elm Street’ teen-age exploitation horror films. ‘If we drop this record (i.e., play this song) and the crowd don’t go wild, I think we pretty much had it, pally wally,’ he said.” The parenthetical explanation is in the original article.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest example we’ve seen for “drop” used more broadly, in the sense of something new being released, is from a holiday shopping article in the Philadelphia Daily News, Sept. 16, 1993:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“New albums drop like leaves in the fall, as the holiday shopping season approaches. CDs and tapes make great Christmas or Hanukkah gifts because they’re (relatively) cheap, you don’t have to worry about anybody’s size.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the early etymology, the Old English noun (<em>dropa</em>) and verb (<em>dropian</em>) ultimately come from the reconstructed prehistoric base <em>dhreu-</em> (to fall, flow, drip, droop), according to the <em>American Heritage Dictionary of Indo-European Roots</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In Old English, the <em>OED</em> says, the noun originally referred to “a small spherical or pear-shaped portion of liquid.” The plural <em>dropan</em> is used in the dictionary’s first citation for the noun:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"> “His swat wæs swylce blodes dropan” (“His sweat was like drops of blood”). From Luke in the <em>West Saxon Gospels</em>, dating from the late 900’s.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Oxford</em> defines the original Old English verb as “to fall in drops; to drip or trickle down.” The third-person plural <em>dropiað</em> is used in the dictionary’s first citation:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Myrre and gutta and cassia dropiað of þinum claðum” (“Myrrh and aloes and cassia droppeth from your garments”). From a late ninth-century prose translation of the Latin Psalms, traditionally attributed to King Alfred.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: How can we get everyone to quit using “loose” when they mean “lose”? It&#8217;s driving me insane!</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The word “lose” is usually a verb with the sense of failing to win or hold on to something, while “loose” is usually an adjective meaning not securely attached, or less commonly a verb meaning to release or set free.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But as you’ve noticed, some people spell “lose” with an extra “o” in casual writing, a usage that some language references suggest may be influenced by the double “o” in “choose,” a word that rhymes with “lose.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Fowler’s Dictionary of Modern English Usage</em> (4th ed.), for example, says, “Possibly because <em>lose</em> is pronounced like <em>choose</em>, there is a common tendency to write that meaning with two letter <em>o</em>s.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Similarly, the <em>Merriam-Webster</em> online dictionary says, “the fact that <em>lose</em> rhymes so well with <em>choose</em> seems to prompt many people to assume that it too should contain a second <em>O</em>.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Nevertheless, the spelling of “lose” as “loose” is relatively rare in contemporary books, newspapers, magazines, and other edited writing, both online and off. <em>Garner’s Modern English Usage</em> (4th ed.) describes it as Stage 1, the earliest level, in its Language Change Index.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Interestingly, the “oo” usage was fairly common in the early Modern English of the late 16th and 17th centuries, when Shakespeare, Milton, Bacon, and other writers used “loose” for “lose.” Here are a few examples from the <em>Oxford English Dictionary:</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">•  “At length, the left winge of the Arcadians began to loose ground” (from <em>The Countesse of Pembrokes Arcadia,</em> 1590, by Sir Philip Sidney).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><b>•  </b>“I thanke thee, thou shalt not loose by it” (from <em>The Taming of the Shrew</em>, written in the early 1590s by Shakespeare). We’ve expanded the cited comment by Petruchio in Act 1, Scene 2, one of many passages in Shakespeare’s plays that use “loose” in the modern sense of “lose.&#8221;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><b>•  </b>“As to a monoculos [one-eyed man] it is more to loose one eye, then to a man that hath two eyes” (<em>Of Coulers of Good &amp; Euill</em>, 1597, by Francis Bacon). In the title, “coulers” (colors) refers to questionable arguments or reasons.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><b>•  </b>“The tempting stream, with one small drop to loose / In sweet forgetfulness all pain and woe” (<em>Paradise Lost</em>, 1667, by Milton). We’ve expanded the citation, which describes the desire of the fallen angels to drink from the river Lethe and lose their torment in forgetfulness.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Our standard spelling and pronunciation of “lose” and “loose” evolved in Old English, Middle English, and early Modern English, according to the <em>OED</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When “lose” first appeared in Old English, it was spelled <em>losien</em>, according to the dictionary,  but in Middle English it was variously <em>losie, losien, losse, loss, loose, </em>and<em> lose.</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for “loose,” it was originally <em>leowsin</em> in Middle English, then<em> lauce, laus, lowss, loyse, lous, lose, loiss, losyn, louce, louss, loss, looce, looze, los, loase, lows, lowis, lewce, leuse, </em>and <em>loose. </em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">By the middle to late 18th century, language authorities had recognized the standard modern spelling and pronunciation, but much of the population hadn’t got the word.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In <em>A Dictionary of the English Language</em> (1755), Samuel Johnson spelled “lose” and “loose” the now-standard ways in their various senses.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in <em>A Critical Pronouncing Dictionary and Expositor of the English Language</em> (1791), John Walker pronounced “lose” as LOOZ, and “loose” as LOOS. Walker uses an inverted circumflex over each “o” to indicate a “slender o,” like those in “do” and “soon.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But a century later, language authorities were still commenting on the spelling of “lose” as “loose.” The entry for “lose” in <em>The Century Dictionary</em> (1889), for example, describes the verb as “formerly also <em>loose</em>” and “more or less confused with <em>loose</em>, untie, relax.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And yet another century later, <em>Merriam-Webster’s Dictionary of English Usage </em>(1994), points out that “this error is rare in edited material but fairly common in casual writing. A quick look in any dictionary is all that is needed to avoid it.”</p>

		<title>A thorny question?</title>
		<link>https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/03/a-thorny-question.html?utm_source=rss&#038;utm_medium=rss&#038;utm_campaign=a-thorny-question</link>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>Q: How was the definite article that we now see in the faux-archaic names of ye olde shoppes actually pronounced in Old English and Middle English when it was written with a thorn? A: The article “the” was originally spelled se in Old English and pronounced like a clipped version of “say” without the glide [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/03/a-thorny-question.html">A thorny question?</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: How was the definite article that we now see in the faux-archaic names of ye olde shoppes actually pronounced in Old English and Middle English when it was written with a thorn?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The article “the” was originally spelled <em>se</em> in Old English and pronounced like a clipped version of “say” without the glide at the end. That spelling and its variants continued into Middle English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the Old English article was also spelled <em>ðe</em> and <em>þe</em> during much of this period. The Anglo-Saxon runes eth (<em>ð</em>) and thorn (<em>þ</em>) were pronounced “th,” so <em>ðe</em> and <em>þe</em> did indeed sound like our word “the.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first example for the article “the” in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> is from an early ninth-century will in the Kentish dialect of early Old English:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Æðelnoð se gerefa to Eastorege” (“Æðelnoð the reeve at Eastry”). From “Will of Æðelnoð &amp; Gænburg,<em>”</em> <em>Anglo-Saxon Charters</em> (1956), by Agnes Jane Robertson.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first example in the <em>OED</em> for the definite article spelled with a “th” sound is from an Old English gloss, or translation, inserted in the 10th century between the lines of Latin in the <em>Lindisfarne Gospels.</em></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The Anglo-Saxon scribe who wrote the gloss translated the Latin “<em>Herodes rex</em>” in Matthew 2:3 with an eth rune, as “Herodes ðe cynig” (“Herod the King”). The Latin itself dates from the eighth century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>OED</em> example written with a thorn is from a gloss inserted during the 10th century in the eighth-century Latin of Matthew 2:9 in the Rushworth Gospels.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The scribe translated the Latin “Stella quam uiderant in oriente antecedebat eos” as “þe steorra þe hiae ær gesægon in eastdæle foreeade hię” (“The star that they had seen earlier in the east went before them”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>OED</em> example for &#8220;ye” used in the sense of &#8220;the&#8221; appeared in the Middle English of the late 14th century. It&#8217;s from a 1389 list of ordinances governing guilds in Norwich, England:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“alle ye bretheren and sisteren” (<em>English Gilds: The Original Ordinances of More Than One Hundred Early English Gilds</em>, 1892, by Joshua Toulmin Smith and Lucy Toulmin Smith).</p>
<p>Why was the article written as &#8220;ye&#8221; here, not <em>þe</em>, and how was it pronounced? When the  printing press arrived in England in the 15th century, printers didn&#8217;t have metal type for a thorn and substituted the letter &#8220;y,&#8221; but &#8220;ye&#8221; was pronounced as &#8220;the.&#8221; That use of &#8220;ye&#8221; for &#8220;the&#8221; probably inspired the faux-archaic names of ye olde shoppes.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the pronoun “ye,” one of four archaic forms of “you,” it was originally spelled <em>ge</em> in Old English and pronounced like the first two letters of the word “yes.” By early Middle English, it was spelled <em>ȝe</em>, with the “y” sound expressed by the yogh rune (<em>ȝ</em>).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The four ways of expressing “you”-ness in Old English were the singulars “thou” and “thee,” and the plurals “ye” and “you.” The Anglo-Saxons used “thou” and “ye” as subjects, “thee” and “you” as objects.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>OED</em> citation for the pronoun &#8220;ye&#8221; (spelled <em>ge</em> here) is from the epic poem <em>Beowulf</em>,  believed to date from the early 700s: “Hwæt syndon ge searohæbbendra?” (“Who are ye armor-clad weapon-bearers?”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>Oxford</em> example for the <em>ȝe</em> spelling of the “ye” pronoun is from the <em>Ormulum</em> (circa 1175), a collection of early Middle English homilies: “Hu ȝe muȝhenn lakenn godd” (“How ye may sacrifice to God”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first example with the “ye” spelling is from Sir Thomas Malory’s 15th-century prose version of the legends of King Arthur:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“My fayre felawes wete ye wel that I will torne vnto kynge Arthurs party” (“My  fair fellows, know ye well that I will side with King Arthur&#8217;s party”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ll end with the expression “ye gods,” which <em>Oxford</em> says appeared in the 16th century and was “used to express indignation, disbelief, or amazement, esp. in a consciously archaic or grandiose way.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The<em> OED</em>’s first citation is from <em>The Paradox of Marcus Tullius Cicero </em>(circa 1543), a translation by the English grammarian Robert Whittington of Cicero&#8217;s <em>Paradoxa Stoicorum</em> (“Stoic Paradoxes”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the passage cited by the dictionary, Whittington translates the Latin “O di immortales<em>”</em> as “O ye goddes immortall.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">(Note: All the senses of “ye” mentioned in this post are now pronounced YEE.)</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: In a NY Times obituary, a historian refers to Ayatollah Ali Khamenei as “arrogant, literate, obdurate, revengeful,” etc. Is it not odd to describe an Islamic scholar as “literate,” i.e., merely able to read and write?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The word “literate” meant educated or learned when it first appeared in English in the 14th century. Although the primary meaning now is able to read or write, you can still find the original sense in standard dictionaries.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Merriam-Webster</em> includes “educated,” “cultured,” “literary”; <em>American Heritage</em>, “familiar with literature,” “literary”; <em>Cambridge</em>, “having a good education or showing it in your writing”; <em>Collins</em>, “intelligent and well-educated”; <em>Webster’s New World</em>, “well-educated,” “showing extensive knowledge, learning, or culture.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So one could defend the use of  “literate” by Abbas Milani, director of Iranian studies at Stanford University, in the Feb. 28, 2026, Times <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/28/world/middleeast/ayatollah-ali-khamenei-dead.html?searchResultPosition=6">obituary</a>. But we would have used “learned” or a similar word to avoid confusion with today’s general meaning of the adjective.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for its etymology, “literate” originally meant “acquainted with letters or literature; erudite, learned, lettered,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>’s earliest citation refers to “a c. childer litterate” (“a hundred literate children”). From <em>Polychronicon</em>, John Trevisa’s translation, sometime before 1387, of an earlier 14th-century work by Ranulf Higden.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the early 17th century, <em>Oxford</em> says, “literate” took on its “weakened” use—“able to read and write”—which is “now the usual sense.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first citation, which we’ve expanded, is from <em>The Description of Penbrokshire</em>,<em> </em>by George Owen of Henllys, Lord of Kemes (composed in 1602-1603 and first published in 1892):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“to indorse the wittnesses names on the backe of the deede, and this to be done by the wittnesses themselves, if they were literate; otherwise by some clerke or other for them.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> describes this sense of “literate” as “opposed to <em>illiterat</em>e,” which had appeared a half-century earlier. The two terms are derived from the Latin <em>litteratus</em>  (lettered, learned) and <em>illiteratus</em> (unlettered, unlearned).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s the dictionary’s earliest “illiterate” citation: “No more can Iudgis, Illitturate Discus ane mater” (“No more can illiterate judges discuss [adjudicate] any matter”). From <em>Ane Compendious and Breue Tractate</em> (1556), a tract by the Church of Scotland minister William Lauder on the duty of kings, pastors, and judges.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Getting back to “literate,” the adjective took on a new sense in the 20th century: “competent or knowledgeable in a particular area,” such as as “politically literate,” “culturally literate,” or “computer literate.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first example in the <em>OED</em> for this sense is from a US newspaper: “The American people were busy becoming ‘politically literate’; that is, learning how to run their political system” (Boston Daily Globe, March 23, 1919).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation for “computer literate” is from a 1961 lecture by the British novelist and chemist C. P. Snow about the danger of relying on computers and computer adepts to make decisions:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“It is going to take a major effort to make all scientific administrators computer-literate. It is going to be quite impossible within foreseeable time to make nonscientific administrators computer-literate.” From a report in Technology Review (July 1961) on “Men and Machines,” a conference at MIT.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Robert Herrick uses “ye” during most of “To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time,” but switches to “you” at the end. Are both ”ye” and “you” in the subjective case in the poem?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Yes, both “ye” and “you” are subjects in “To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time,” a 17th-century poem famous for its opening lines:</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Gather ye rose-buds while ye may,<br />
Old time is still a-flying;<br />
And this same flower that smiles today,<br />
Tomorrow will be dying.</p>
</blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But as you’ve noticed, he uses “you” only once—in the last line of the last stanza:</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Then be not coy, but use your time,<br />
And while ye may, go marry;<br />
For having lost but once your prime,<br />
You may forever tarry.</p>
</blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As is turns out, our modern word “you” originally had four principal forms. In Old English, the subjects were “ye” (plural) and “thou” (singular); the objects were “you” (plural) and “thee” (singular).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But by the time Herrick was writing, “you” had become the standard second-person pronoun in English, whether subject or object, singular or plural. Although “ye,” “thee,” and “thou” were archaic by then (along with the possessives “thy” and &#8220;thine”), they were still seen in poetry and religious writing.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As the British linguist David Crystal explains in <em>The Stories of English </em>(2004), the rules for using the second-person pronouns began to change in Middle English: “The first change was the emergence of <em>you</em> as a singular, noticeably during the second half of the thirteenth century.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A second change, Crystal writes, took place during the 16th century, when “the difference between the subject and object forms gradually disappeared, and <em>you</em> became the norm in both situations. <em>Ye</em> was still used at the end of the century, but only in contexts which were somewhat literary, religious, or archaic.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> notes that “you” has “long been the invariable form as both subject and object form in almost all contexts in the modern standard language.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So why does Herrick switch from “ye” to “you” in the last line of “To the Virgins”? He may have considered “you” (the standard form in the early Modern English of the 17th century) more forceful than the poetic “ye,” and better at emphasizing his warning.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, here’s the full poem, with its original spelling and long “s” (ſ), from <em>Hesperides: or, the Works, Both Humane and Divine of Robert Herrick Esq.</em> (1648):</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>To the Virgins, to make much of Time.<br />
</em><br />
Gather ye Roſe-buds while ye may,<br />
Old Time is ſtill a flying :<br />
And this ſame flower that ſmiles to day,<br />
To morrow will be dying.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The glorious Lamp of Heaven the Sun,<br />
The higher he’s a getting ;<br />
The ſooner will his Race be run,<br />
And neerer he&#8217;s to Setting.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">That Age is beſt, which is the firſt,<br />
When Youth and Blood are warmer ;<br />
But being ſpent, the worſe, and worſt<br />
Times, ſtill ſucceed the former.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Then be not coy, but uſe your time ;<br />
And while ye may, goe marry :<br />
For having loſt but once your prime,<br />
You may for ever tarry.</p>
</blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: In Jen Beagin’s 2023 novel <em>Big Swiss</em>, Flavia asks Om, her sex therapist, whether “adult” and “adultery” are related. He says they aren’t. Huh? Could that be right?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"> A: Yes. Both English words ultimately come from different classical Latin sources: “adult” from <em>adultus</em> (full-grown, mature), and “adultery” from <em>adulterare</em> (to commit adultery, debase, counterfeit, or corrupt).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first of these terms to appear in English, “adultery,” was recorded in the early 14th century, according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>. The earliest <em>OED</em> citation is from a statute, dated sometime before 1325, in <em>A Middle English Statute-Book</em> (2011), edited by Claire Fennell:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Ant te womman, ȝif heo mide hire oune wille forsok hire hosebonde, ant tuuelde in hire aduoterie, a sal lusen aremanaund accion to purchasen hire dowere of þe tenement of þe foreseide man þat was hire lord.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">(“And the woman, if she of her own free will forsook her husband and lived in adultery, she shall lose forever the right of action to claim her dower [a widow’s share of her husband’s estate] from the holding of the aforementioned man who was her lord.”)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>Oxford</em> citation for the adjective “adult” is from a treatise on how to train statesmen: “Soche persons, beinge nowe adulte, that is to saye, passed theyr childehode” (<em>The Boke Named the Gouernour</em>, 1531, by the English scholar and diplomat Thomas Elyot).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The noun “adult” appeared a century later. The <em>OED</em> example is a from a treatise critical of Anabaptism, the doctrine that baptism should be administered only to believing adults.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In this passage from<em> Anabaptism Routed</em> (1655), John Reading argues that being baptized as a baby provides a stronger Christian foundation than later baptism:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“In the adult, coming to the knowledge of God’s covenant in Christ, and of his own feeling in infancy, it must make him more confident of his implantation into Christ, then if he knew that he never had been baptized.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">To get back to your question, no, “adult” and “adultery” are not related, but only the former can commit the latter.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Having been sucked down many a “rabbit hole” in my reading, I’m wondering how this figurative sense of the phrase developed. Did it appear before <em>Alice in Wonderland</em> was published?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The phrase “rabbit hole” has been used both literally and figuratively for hundreds of years, well before Alice fell down one. Although you might have expected it to be used literally first, the earliest recorded usage seems to be figurative.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first citation in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> criticizes a religious writer who “makes breaches and Rabbet holes to pop in as he please” (<em>A Dissuasive From Popery</em>, 1667, by Jeremy Taylor, a Church of Ireland bishop).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>’s earliest literal example is from a 1705 description of Dunmore Cave in County Kilkenny, Ireland:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The earth turned up at the entrance of a rabbit-hole” (<em>The Works of George Berkeley</em>, Vol. IV, 1871, edited by Alexander Campbell Fraser). Berkeley was a philosopher and Church of Ireland bishop.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So what was a rabbit burrow referred to before that? The usual term was “coney hole.” An adult rabbit used to be called a “coney” while a young one was a “rabbit.” (The <em>OED</em> says the term “coney” is now obsolete.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest “coney hole” example is from <em>Hortus Vocabulorum</em> (1500),  a Latin-English glossary: “a conyes hole.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The best-known literal example for “rabbit hole” is from <em>Alice’</em><em>s Adventures in Wonderland</em> (1865), by Lewis Carrol. In the novel, Alice follows a white rabbit “down a large rabbit-hole” into a magical world.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the 20th century, the <em>OED</em> says, the children’s book inspired the use of “rabbit hole” in the modern figurative sense: &#8220;to indicate passage into a strange, surreal, or nonsensical situation or environment,” chiefly in the expression “<em>down a</em> (also <em>the</em>) <em>rabbit hole</em>.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first citation refers to a legal rabbit hole: “It is the Rabbit-Hole down which we fell into the Law, and to him who has gone down it, no queer performance is strange” (Yale Law Journal, 1938).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And we found this more recent example in a New York Times headline: “Going Down the Junk Food Rabbit Hole” (Oct. 27, 2025).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for “rabbit,” it first appeared in Middle English in the 13th century, apparently derived from Anglo-Norman or Middle French.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>Oxford </em>citation is from John Trevisa’s translation of <em>De Proprietatibus Rerum</em> (“On the Properties of Things”), an encyclopedic Latin work compiled around 1240 by Bartholomeus Anglicus:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Conynges … bringen forþ many rabettes &amp; multiplien ful swiþe” (“Coneys … bring forth many rabbits and multiply very quickly”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>OED</em> example for “coney” is from the Middle English of the late 12th century: “Ne sal þar ben foh, ne grai, ne cunin” (“There shall be no mottled [fur], nor grey [fur], nor coney [fur]”). From “Poema Morale” (circa 1175).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But if the words “coney” and “rabbit” didn’t turn up until Middle English, what was a rabbit called in Old English (roughly 450 to 1150)? As it turns out, the Anglo-Saxons didn’t have a word for the rabbit (whether old or young) because the animal was apparently unknown to them. Here’s how the <em>OED</em> explains this:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Although there is archaeological evidence to suggest that rabbits existed in Britain before the last ice age and that some attempt may have been made to reintroduce them in the Roman period, the rabbit appears to have been unknown to the Anglo-Saxons, and only successfully re-established in Norman times.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Any Anglo-Saxons who did somehow come across a rabbit might have referred to it as a “hare,” a larger herbivore of the genus Lepus that was common in Britain during the time of Beowulf.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I am wondering how <a href="https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/chimera">chimera</a> has come to mean both “an imaginary monster compounded of incongruous parts” and “an unrealizable dream.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: When “chimera” originally appeared in ancient Greece as χίμαιρα (<em>khimaira</em>), it also had two meanings—a female goat as well as a monster. In its farming sense, χίμαιρα was simply the female version of a male goat, χίμαρος (<em>khimaros</em>).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the <em>Iliad</em>, which dates from the seventh century BC, Homer describes the monster as πρόσθε λέων, ὄπιθεν δὲ δράκων, μέσση δὲ χίμαιρα, δεινὸν ἀποπνείουσα πυρὸς μένος αἰθομένοιο (“in front a lion, in back a serpent, and in the middle a she-goat, breathing out terribly with the might of a blazing fire”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When Middle English adopted the term in the late 14th century from the Middle French <em>chimère</em> and the Latin <em>chimaera</em>, it was spelled “chymere” and referred to the monster of Greek mythology, according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>. The earliest English citation in the <em>OED</em> is from the prologue of the Wycliffe Bible (early version, 1382):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Beestis clepid chymeres, that han a part of ech beest, and suche ben not, no but oonly in opynyoun” (“Beasts called chimeras, that have parts of other beasts, exist only in the mind”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> says “chimera” (now pronounced kigh-MIR-uh or kuh-MIR-uh) took on several figurative meanings in the 16th century “with reference to the terrible character, the unreality, or the incongruous composition of the fabled monster.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the early 16th century, for example, it came to mean “a horrible and fear-inspiring phantasm, a bogy.” The first <em>Oxford</em> citation for this sense is from an English adaptation of a Latin pastoral poem:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Agayne [Against] the Chymer [Chimera], here stoutly must he fight.” (From the Fourth Eclogue in <em>The Boke of Codrus and Mynalcas</em>, by Alexander Barclay, based on a 15th-century Latin poem by the Carmelite monk Baptista Mantuanus.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the late 16th century, the <em>OED</em> says, the term took on another figurative sense: “an unreal creature of the imagination, a mere wild fancy; an unfounded conception.” The dictionary describes this sense as “the ordinary modern use.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>Oxford</em> citation is from an English translation of Philippe de Mornay’s French defense of Christianity: “How could that Chymera haue come in any mannes mynd?” From <em>A Woorke Concerning the Trewnesse of the Christian Religion </em>(1587), by Philip Sidney and Arthur Golding.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">(Golding finished the translation the year after Sidney, a poet, scholar, and soldier, was fatally wounded at the age of 31 in a battle with Spanish forces.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the adjectives, “chimerical” appeared in the early 17th century and the less common “chimeric” a couple of decades later.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em> has two senses for “chimerical” (kigh-MIR-ik-uhl or kuh-MIR-ik-uhl):</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“1. Of the nature of a chimera; vainly or fantastically conceived, imaginary, fanciful, visionary” and “2. Prone to entertain chimeras; filled with idle fancies and wild dreams; whimsical, fanciful.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s the dictionary’s first adjectival citation: “The fire of Purgatory is rightly termed … Chymericall, because a meere fiction.” From <em>A Case for the Spectacles, or, A Defence of Via Tuta, the Safe Way</em> (1638), by the Church of England clergyman and religious controversialist Daniel Featley.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>, an etymological dictionary, similarly defines “chimeric” (kigh-MEHR-ik or kuh-MEHR-ik). <em>Merriam-Webster</em>, a standard dictionary that reflects current usage, says “chimeric” is usually used now in two senses in genetics:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“a: relating to, derived from, or being a genetic <em>chimera</em>: containing tissue with two or more genetically distinct populations of cells. ‘<em>Chimeric</em>  mice are generated by mixing cells from two embryos, generating a mouse with four parental contributions’—Nancy L. Nadon [gerontologist].</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“b: composed of material (such as DNA or polypeptide) from more than one organism. ‘He combined this gene material with safer human DNA and created what’s known as a <em>chimeric</em> gene—a gene of antibody instructions that is part man, part mouse’—Eric Hand [science journalist].”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Getting back to your question, it’s not at all surprising that a word like “chimera” would develop various figurative meanings. Many other terms from Greek mythology have metaphorical senses in English. Here are some of them: “Achilles’ heel” (a vulnerable point), “mentor” (an experienced adviser), “Pandora’s box” (a source of troubles), and “odyssey” (a long and eventful journey, from the character Odysseus).</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I was reading an op-ed that had this quote from Abraham Lincoln’s Cooper Union Address: “That is cool.” At first I thought it was satire, but he did indeed say this. What did he mean by “cool”?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Lincoln used “cool” in his 1860 Cooper Union speech to mean impudent or shameless, senses that appeared in two of the leading American dictionaries of the time, though the primary meaning was, as it is now, “moderately cold” or “not warm.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The entry for the adjective “cool” in <em>An American Dictionary of the English Language</em> (1860), by Noah Webster, revised and enlarged by Chauncey A. Goodrich, includes this sense: “Impudent in a high degree, as when speaking of some trick, pretension, &amp;c., we say, ‘that is <em>cool</em>.’ ”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And the entry for the adjective in <em>A Dictionary of the English Language</em> (1860), by Joseph Emerson Worcester, includes this sense: “Shameless; impudent. [Colloquial.]” It cites this example from Punch: “That struck me as rather cool.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference, says the “impudent” sense of “cool” first appeared in the early 18th century and described “a person, an action, or a person’s behaviour: assured and unabashed where diffidence and hesitation would be expected; composedly and deliberately audacious or impudent in making a proposal, demand, or assumption.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>OED</em> citation for this sense is from the English writer Aaron Hill’s tragedy <em>King Henry V </em>(1723), an adaptation of Shakespeare’s 1599 play that adds a romance between the king’s mistress and the French dauphin. The mistress complains of the king’s “cool Insolence of Pride, and Majesty.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In Lincoln’s Feb. 27, 1860, speech at what was then the Cooper Institute, he compares the threat of Southern Democrats to secede from the Union if a Republican were elected president to an armed robbery:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“In that supposed event, you say, you will destroy the Union; and then, you say, the great crime of having destroyed it will be upon us! That is cool. A highwayman holds a pistol to my ear, and mutters through his teeth, ‘Stand and deliver, or I shall kill you, and then you will be a murderer!’ ”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the informal sense of “cool” that came to mind when you first noticed the term in Lincoln’s speech, the <em>OED</em> describes it as “originally in African American usage: (as a general term of approval) admirable, excellent.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s earliest citation is from “The Gilded Six-Bits,” a short story by Zora Neale Hurston: “And whut make it so cool, he got money ’cumulated. And womens give it all to ’im” (Story magazine, August 1933).</p>

We also posted about the very old noun “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2013/07/coolth.html">coolth</a>” in 2013.]</p>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>Q: Here’s the title of a post on a blog I follow: “More osculation of religion by the NYT and Free Press.” I’m not aware of this figurative use of “osculation,” but it could be ignorance on my part. 										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Here’s the title of a post on a blog I follow: “More osculation of religion by the NYT and Free Press.” I’m not aware of this figurative use of “osculation,” but it could be ignorance on my part.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: “Osculation” is being used here to mean “kissing,” the original sense of the English noun and its Latin ancestor. However, the noun is now used humorously in its kissing sense, or used as a mathematical term for the point at which a pair of curves or surfaces touch.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The evolutionary biologist Jerry A. Coyne, a religious skeptic, is using “osculation” satirically on his website <a href="https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2026/01/19/more-osculation-of-religion-by-the-nyt-and-free-press/">Why Evolution Is True</a> to say The New York Times and The Free Press are kissing up to religion by taking it seriously.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">English borrowed the noun “osculation” and the verb “osculate” from Latin in the mid-17th century. Both terms ultimately come from <em>osculum</em>, Latin for a &#8220;kiss” (literally, a “little mouth,” the diminutive of <em>os</em>, or “mouth”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> defines “osculation” as “the action of kissing; a kiss.” The earliest <em>OED</em> citation is from <em>The New World of English Words</em> (1658), by Edward Phillips: “<em>Osculation</em>, a kissing or imbracing.” Phillips was a nephew of Milton and educated by him.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the verb, the <em>OED</em> defines it as “to kiss (a person or thing), to salute with contact of the lips.” It labels the usage “now <em>archaic</em> or <em>humorous</em>.” The dictionary’s first example is from a dictionary of difficult words:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“<em>Osculate</em>, to kiss, to love heartily, to imbrace.” (From <em>Glossographia: or, a Dictionary, Interpreting All Such Hard Words, Whether Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Italian, Spanish, French, Teutonick, Belgick, British or Saxon, as Are Now Used in Our Refined English Tongue</em>, 1665, by the antiquary and lexicographer Thomas Blount.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the mathematics sense, the <em>OED</em> says the verb appeared first in the 18th century and meant “to touch (another curve or surface) so as to have a common tangent at the point of contact.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first citation is from <em>Cyclopædia; or, an Universal Dictionary of Arts and Sciences</em> (1728), by Ephraim Chambers:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Circle described on the Point C, as a Centre … with the Radius of the Evolute M C, is said to osculate, kiss, the Curve described by Evolution in M; which Point M is call’d by the Inventor [Christiaan] Huygens, the Osculum of the Curve.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The<em> </em>math sense of the noun appeared in the 19th century. The <em>OED</em> says the noun here refers to “contact of curves or surfaces which share a common tangent at the point of contact.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary’s first citation is from <em>An Elementary Treatise on the Differential and Integral Calculus</em> (1816), an English translation of <em>Traité Élémentaire du Calcul Différentiel et du Calcul Intégral</em> (1802), by the French mathematician Silvestre François Lacroix:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The contact of the highest order which can take place between the tangent curve, and the one proposed, depends on the number of constants which the equation of the former involves, and is also called the contact of osculation.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Getting back to the original kissing sense, the <em>OED</em> has this 20th-century example of “osculating” from <em>Don&#8217;t Tread on Me: the Selected Letters of S. J. Perelman</em> (1987), edited by Prudence Crowther:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“When I asked him once why his hands were so cold he said, ‘They&#8217;ve been insufficiently osculated’ ” (from Crowther&#8217;s introduction).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, in anticipation of Valentine’s Day, a small toast to the original sense of “osculation”—the opening lines of “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0X2wK5zUdrQ">As Time Goes By</a>”:</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">You must remember this, a kiss is still a kiss,<br />
A sigh is just a sigh;<br />
The fundamental things apply,<br />
As time goes by.</p>
</blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Can euphemisms turn into dysphemisms and vice versa? If yes, why does it happen?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Yes, euphemisms can turn into dysphemisms, and vice versa. The change from a euphemism to its opposite is referred to as pejoration (worsening), while the change from a dysphemism to a euphemism is called amelioration (bettering).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The linguist Stephen Pinker has described this phenomenon as “the euphemism treadmill” (“The Game of the Name,” The New York Times, April 5, 1994).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“People invent new ‘polite’ words to refer to emotionally laden or distasteful things,” Pinker writes, “but the euphemism becomes tainted by association and the new one that must be found acquires its own negative connotations.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In all this vice-versa-ing, he says, “ ‘Water closet’ becomes ‘toilet’ (originally a term for any body care, as in ‘toilet kit’), which becomes ‘bathroom,’ which becomes ‘rest room,’ which becomes ‘lavatory.’  ‘Garbage collection’ turns into ‘sanitation,’ which turns into ‘environmental services.’ ”</p>
<p>We’ll add a few adjectives that have evolved from dysphemisms to euphemisms: “fond” and “nice” originally meant foolish or silly, while “shrewd” meant depraved or wicked, and &#8220;sophisticated” meant adulterated, according to the <i>Oxford English Dictionary</i>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The euphemism treadmill shows that concepts, not words, are in charge: give a concept a new name, and the name becomes colored by the concept,” Pinker writes, “the concept does not become freshened by the name. (We will know we have achieved equality and mutual respect when names for minorities stay put.)”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In <em>Forbidden Words: Taboo and the Censoring of Language</em> (2006), Keith Allan and Kate Burridge coin the term “X-phemism,” a collective word for euphemisms, dysphemisms, and orthophemisms (their term for neutral words).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Allan and Burridge discuss “the concept of <em>cross-varietal synonymy</em>, i.e. words that have the same meaning as other words used in different contexts. For instance, the X-phemisms <em>poo</em>, <em>shit</em>, and <em>faeces</em> are cross-varietal synonyms because they denote the same thing but have different connotations, which mark different styles used in different circumstances.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We assume you’ve seen our 2016 post “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2016/08/dysphemism-euphemism.html">On dysphemism and euphemism</a>.” If not, check it out.</p>

		<title>When ‘misery’ rhymes with ‘high’</title>
		<link>https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/01/misery.html?utm_source=rss&#038;utm_medium=rss&#038;utm_campaign=misery</link>
		
		<dc:creator><![CDATA[Pat and Stewart]]></dc:creator>
		<pubDate>Mon, 26 Jan 2026 13:10:22 +0000</pubDate>

		<category><![CDATA[English language]]></category>

		<category><![CDATA[Expression]]></category>
		<category><![CDATA[Language]]></category>
		<category><![CDATA[Linguistics]]></category>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>Q: Was it ever normal to rhyme “misery” and “high”? I’m thinking of a couplet (“Make safe the way that leads on high, / And close the path to misery”) in the hymn “O Come, O Come, Emmanuel.” A: The noun “misery” has been pronounced various ways since it first appeared in the Middle English [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2026/01/misery.html">When ‘misery’ rhymes with ‘high’</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Was it ever normal to rhyme “misery” and “high”? I’m thinking of a couplet (“Make safe the way that leads on high, / And close the path to misery”) in the hymn “O Come, O Come, Emmanuel.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: The noun “misery” has been pronounced various ways since it first appeared in the Middle English of the late 14th century, but the most common were roughly similar to the way the word is pronounced now (MIZ-uh-ree).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In Middle English, the noun (usually spelled “miserie&#8221; or “myserie”) was pronounced MIZ-uh-ree or MIZ-uh-ree-uh, according to the University of Michigan’s  <em>Middle English Dictionary</em>. (We’ve used capital letters to indicate stress.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>’s earliest citation is from <em>The Monk’s Tale</em> (circa 1375), by Chaucer: “Now artow Sathanas, that mayst nat twynne / Out of miserie in which thou art falle” (“Now art thou Satan, that mayest not depart / Out of the misery in which thou art fallen”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the early modern English of the late 16th and early 17th centuries, “misery” was still usually pronounced MIZ-uh-ree, but it could be MIZ-uh-rye (rhyming with “high”) in poetry and formal speech, according to <em>English Pronunciation 1500–1700</em> (2nd ed., 1998), by the Oxford philologist Eric John Dobson.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Dobson, citing contemporary orthoepists (pronunciation and spelling experts), suggests that poets and orators often pronounced the last syllable in words like “misery” with a diphthongized long “i” (sounding like “eye”) to differentiate terms of Old French and Latin origin from those of Anglo-Saxon and Germanic origin.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In <em>The Faerie Queene</em> (1590), Edmund Spenser rhymes the plural “miseries” with the verb “plies.” Here Una has found a haven among satyrs and fauns after wandering alone in the wilderness: “And a long time with that salvage people she staid, / To gather breath in many miseries. / During which time her gentle wit she plyes.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Similarly, Shakespeare rhymes the plural “miseries” with “eyes” in <em>Henry VI, Part 1</em> (written about 1591). Here the Duke of Bedford is speaking to others in the funeral procession of King Henry V: “Away with these disgraceful wailing robes. / Wounds will I lend the French instead of eyes / To weep their intermissive miseries.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As far as we can tell, Shakespeare rhymes only the plural of “misery,” but he has many singular examples with other words ending in “y.” In <em>A Midsummer Night&#8217;s Dream</em>, for example, Oberon rhymes “archery,” “gloriously,” and “remedy” with “dye,” “eye,” “espy,” “sky,” and “by” as he applies nectar to the eyes of Demetrius:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Flower of this purple dye, / Hit with Cupid’s archery, / Sink in apple of his eye. / When his love he doth espy, / Let her shine as gloriously / As the Venus of the sky. — /  When thou wak’st, if she be by, / Beg of her for remedy.&#8221;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the poetry and formal speech of the 18th century, the final “y” of many words of three or more syllables (like “misery”) continued to be rhymed with words clearly ending in an “eye” sound, never mind that most people pronounced the final “y” in these polysyllabic terms with an “ee” sound.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In “An Essay on Man” (1733-34), a philosophical poem, Alexander Pope rhymes “company” with “sky”: “But thinks, admitted to that equal sky / His faithful Dog shall bear him company.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in “The Tyger&#8221; (1794), William Blake rhymes “symmetry” with “eye”: “Tyger Tyger, burning bright, / In the forests of the night; / What immortal hand or eye, Could frame thy fearful symmetry?”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The British linguist David Crystal says the pronunciation of “symmetry” with a diphthong at the end  “would have sounded distinctly old-fashioned by then. But wouldn&#8217;t that suit someone who begins a poem with a spelling of <em>tyger</em> that was also archaic?” (<a href="https://tinyurl.com/2nrmvbvx">DC blog</a>, Sept. 9, 2013).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Crystal notes that the lexicographer and philologist John Walker rhymes “symmetry” with “me” in <em>A Critical Pronouncing Dictionary and Expositor of the English Language</em> (1791). That suggests the SIM-uh-tree pronunciation was standard English at the time.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, some poets continued to use the diphthongized “eye” pronunciation of these “y” words as a sign of respect, especially to emphasize someone or something of venerable age, wisdom, or character. That’s probably why John Mason Neale rhymed “misery” and “high” in the hymn “O Come, O Come, Emmanuel.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Interestingly, Neale produced several different English translations of the Latin hymn “<em>Veni, Veni, Emanuel</em>.” The earliest, “Draw nigh, draw nigh, Emmanuel,” appeared in <em>Mediaeval Hymns and Sequences</em> (1851) and was revised in <em>Hymnal Noted</em> (1852). The better known “O Come, O Come, Emmanuel” appeared in <em>Hymns Ancient and Modern</em> (1861).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The lines you cite were significantly different in the original 1851 version  of the hymn, and read: “Make safe the way that we must go, / And close the path that leads below.” Those lines also appeared in a children’s version, <em>Hymns for the Young</em> (1854).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The wording you’re asking about (“Make safe the way that leads on high, / And close the path to misery”) appeared in the 1852 and 1861 versions of the hymn.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As rhyming in poetry fell out of favor in the 20th century, it was rare to hear the “y” in a word like “misery” pronounced  as a diphthong.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Even a poet like W. H. Auden, who used a fair amount of rhyme, pronounced “poetry” the usual  way (PO-uh-tree) in reading the the first stanza in Section III of “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=73nEk_75jRE">In Memory of W. B. Yeats</a>” (1940):</p>
<blockquote>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Earth, receive an honoured guest:<br />
William Yeats is laid to rest.<br />
Let the Irish vessel lie<br />
Emptied of its poetry.</p>
</blockquote>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I saw this headline over an NPR article: “VP Vance tries to progress Gaza ceasefire.” Is that a permissible use of &#8220;progress&#8221;? I&#8217;m pretty sure I&#8217;ve never seen it used like that, but I&#8217;m slow to pick up on the evolution of English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: That usage is somewhat controversial. The verb &#8220;progress,&#8221; used in that particular way, is frowned upon by standard American dictionaries but is acceptable in British English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ve written about the uses of “progress” as a verb in two of our previous posts—in <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2010/02/progressive-education.html">2010</a> and again in <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2022/09/progress.html">2022</a>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The 2022 post discusses “progress” as an intransitive verb (one without an object) meaning simply to go forward, as in “the construction progressed.” This use is not unusual.</p>
<p>English borrowed the word “progress” from Latin, where <em>progressus </em>referred to a forward movement, an advance, or a development. It appeared as a noun in the 15th century and a verb in the 16th.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We cite many examples from the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em> as we show how the verb progressed over the years. We especially like this one from Shakespeare’s <em>King John</em>, believed written in the mid-1590s:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Let me wipe off this honourable dewe, / That siluerly doth progresse on thy cheekes.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Our 2010 post discusses “progress” in the sense you ask about—as a transitive verb (i.e., one that takes a direct object and means to move <em><u>something</u></em> forward), as in “the builder progressed the construction.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In that post, we answer a question about a transitive use by Sarah Palin in 2008, the year she ran for vice president as the Republican nominee: “Let’s talk about progressing this nation.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As we mentioned, this transitive use is not recognized in American English dictionaries, including <em>American Heritage</em>, <em>Merriam-Webster</em>, <em>Dictionary.com</em>, and <em>Webster&#8217;s New World</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the <em>OED </em>(an etymological dictionary), and the latest editions of <em>Fowler’s Dictionary of Modern English Usage</em> include the transitive use. And so do standard British dictionaries like <em>Cambridge</em>, <em>Collins</em>, and <em>Longman</em>.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I say “fetch” when I want my Lab, Gracie, to retrieve something, but “fetching” may refer to her good looks as well as her retrieving. Am I right to assume the two senses are related?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Yes, both the retrieving and the attractive senses of “fetching” are derived from the verb “fetch,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The story begins in Old English, when “fetch” (originally <em>feccan</em>) meant to go on a quest for someone or something and bring the quarry back.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The verb ultimately comes from <em>ped</em>, the prehistoric Indo-European root for “foot” and “walk,” says <em>The American Heritage Dictionary of Indo-European Roots</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>’s first citation, which refers to fetching a person, is from Ælfric’s translation of Genesis 42:34 in the late 10th century: “Þæt ge þisne eowerne broþur feccon” (“that you should fetch this brother of yours”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Around the same time, the verb was used in reference to fetching an object, as in an <em>OED</em> example from <em>Matthew</em> 24:17 in the West Saxon Gospels.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the passage, Jesus tells his followers that when the apocalypse approaches, a righteous person should flee to the mountains and not go home to fetch anything:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Ne ga he nyðyr þat he ænig þing on his huse fecce” (“Let him not go down to fetch anything in his house”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As far as we can tell, the use of “fetch” in the canine sense first appeared in <em>The Noble Art of Venerie or Hunting</em> (1575), by George Turberville. Here he describes the training of spaniels:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Also it is a good thing to teach them to fetche, and to learne them to mouth a thing gently: for if they teare the fowle, or the thing which they shall fetche, it is a great fault in them.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Soon afterward, the verb was used in reference to the ability of spaniels to retrieve not only waterfowl but bolts (crossbow projectiles) and arrows that had gone astray.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">This example is from <em>Of Englishe Dogges</em>, Abraham Fleming’s 1576 translation of <em>De Canibus Britannicis</em>, a 1570 Latin treatise by John Caius:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“With these dogges also we fetche out of the water such fowle” and “we vse them also to bring vs our boultes &amp; arrowes out of the water (missing our marcke).”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest example we’ve seen for “fetch” used as a command is from <em>Hunger’s Prevention: Or, the Whole Art of Fowling by Water and Land </em>(1655), by Gervase Markham.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In explaining how to teach a dog to retrieve, Markham says one should use a glove to play with the dog, “then cast it further from you and say Fetch.” And if “he doe bring it you make exceeding much of him and reward him either with Bread, or Meate.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for “fetching,” it can be a present participle (as in “Gracie is fetching well today”), a gerund (“Her fetching has been at its best lately”), or an adjective (“She looks especially fetching after being groomed”).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When the adjective first appeared in the 16th century, the <em>OED</em> says, it described someone who “contrives, plans, schemes”—that is, a “crafty, designing” person.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The earliest <em>Oxford</em> example is from <em>Actes and Monumentes,</em> John Foxe’s history of Protestant persecution by Roman Catholics (1570 revised ed.): “What can not the fetchyng practise of the Romishe Prelates bryng aboute?”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">That sense is now obsolete, but in the late 19th century the adjective took on the modern attractive sense, which <em>Oxford</em> defines as “alluring, fascinating, pleasing.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first <em>OED</em> citation, which we’ve expanded to fill an ellipsis, is from <em>Roy and Viola </em>(1880), a Victorian-era novel by “Mrs. Forrester” (pseudonym of Emily Feake Bridges): “there is nothing in the world so fetching as a beautiful voice?”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">How, you’re probably wondering, did “fetching,” an adjective derived from the verb “fetch,” come to mean “crafty” and later “alluring”?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Chambers Dictionary of Etymology</em> suggests that the verb’s original meaning of to pursue and bring back inspired the later “idea of taking or catching one’s attention.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, the adjective “taking” has meant “appealing, engaging, pleasing, charming, captivating” since the 17th century, the <em>OED</em> says, though that sense is “now somewhat <em>dated</em>.”</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I’ve been hearing the word “hopium” used for an imaginary opiate taken to achieve unrealistic optimism, and “copium” used for one taken to endure hard times. I don’t see them in my dictionary. Are they legit?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: Well, one standard dictionary, <em>Collins</em>, recognizes both “hopium” and “copium” while another, <em>Merriam-Webster</em>, recognizes only “copium.” Both dictionaries label these terms slang—that is, informal nonstandard usages.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Collins</em> describes “hopium” as a blend of the words “hope” and “opium,” while it describes “copium” as a blend of “cope” and “opium.” Here are the dictionary’s definitions:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Hopium</em>: “a substance said to have been ingested by those who maintain an unrealistically optimistic outlook.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>Copium</em>: “a substance said to have been ingested by those who remain unduly optimistic in the face of defeat or disappointment.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Are they legit? Well, we’ll coin a slang term of our own to describe “hopium” and “copium” as maybe-ums. We like slang and often use it in informal speech and writing, but only if our audience is likely to understand.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The oldest of these two satirical terms for imaginary opiates, “hopium,” first appeared in the 19th century, but as far as we can tell it didn’t show up again in that sense until the late 20th century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the earliest example we’ve seen, a British satirical weekly (The Tomahawk, May 28, 1870) uses the term in mocking an anti-opiate movement:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“It is possible that Sir Wilfred Lawson may head a great Ante-opiate Movement, the object of which will be to get all people afflicted with toothache to pledge themselves to abstain from laudanum. It will, of course, be marshalled under the title of The Band of Hope-ium!”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The next appearance that we know of is from a book about the 1978 mass suicide and murder of Jim Jones and members of his Peoples Temple religious cult at Jonestown in Guyana:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“This was the practical God in distinction to Sky Gods, Spooks, Buzzard Gods, and the unknown God worshipped by those who were addicted to the ‘hopium’ of myth” (<em>Salvation and Suicide: An Interpretation of Jim Jones, the Peoples Temple, and Jonestown</em>, 1988, by David Chidester).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The usage began appearing more frequently in the early 21st century, perhaps influenced by the “Hope” poster used in Barrack Obama’s 2008 presidential campaign.</p>
<p><a href="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Barack_Obama_Hope_poster.jpg"><img fetchpriority="high" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-medium wp-image-33566" src="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Barack_Obama_Hope_poster-200x300.jpg" alt="" width="200" height="300" srcset="https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Barack_Obama_Hope_poster-200x300.jpg 200w, https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Barack_Obama_Hope_poster.jpg 258w" sizes="(max-width: 200px) 100vw, 200px" /></a></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In “Hopium helps you forget several unpleasant facts,” the headline on a column by John Kass in the Chicago Tribune (July 30, 2008), the term is used satirically for the enthusiasm of Obama’s supporters.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for “copium,” <em>Merriam-Webster</em> describes the term in a “Slang &amp; Trending” feature on its website as “an Internet taunt for a delusional loser,” and has this expansive definition:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“<em>Copium</em> is a slang term for denial or rationalization in the face of defeat or failure. It is presented as a metaphorical drug people take when dealing with losing a game or otherwise being disappointed.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As <em>M-W</em> explains, “The implication of <em>copium </em>is that, rather than honestly accept defeat, someone deals (copes) with it by numbing their pain through denial or the like (as if taking opium).”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary says “various people probably coined <em>copium</em> independently in the 2000s.” The earliest example we&#8217;ve seen is from <em>Copium</em>, a 2003 album by the Oakland, CA, rapper Kreak da Sneak.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We listened to track 6, entitled “Copium,” to find out how the term was used, but the only words we could make out were the numerous “motherfuckers” (and variant forms). We were unable to find a transcription of the lyrics online.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The linguist Ben Zimmer says the title “Copium” here stands for “Counting Other People’s Money” (“Among the New Words,” American Speech, May 2022).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The title on most physical versions of the album is “COPIUM” (all caps), while the title on some others is “Counting Other People’s Money.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In July 2019, the word appeared in a meme on a 4chan forum with an image of the Internet character Pepe the Frog breathing with a mask and tube from a tank labeled “copium.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The meme was used at first to put down political losers who refused to accept defeat. It later came to mean irrational optimism to cope with any kind of disappointment or defeat.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, here’s the image of Pepe, blissed out on copium:</p>
<p><a href="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Copium-3.jpeg"><img decoding="async" class="alignnone size-medium wp-image-33572" src="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Copium-3-300x128.jpeg" alt="" width="300" height="128" srcset="https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Copium-3-300x128.jpeg 300w, https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Copium-3.jpeg 342w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a></p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: After reading  your recent <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2025/11/matriculate.html">article</a> about Hank Stram’s coining a football sense of “matriculate,” I remembered reading a long way back that Stram also coined “Super Bowl.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: No, Hank Stram didn’t coin the term “Super Bowl.” The first person to use it for the football championship game was Lamar Hunt, owner of the Kansas City Chiefs. Interestingly, Hunt had hired Stram, then a little-known assistant football coach at the University of Miami, as the first head coach of the Chiefs.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Hunt used the term on July 15, 1966, in a discussion with Joe McGuff, sports editor of the Kansas City Star, about a merger between the American Football League and the National Football League:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“I think one of the first things we’ll consider is the site of the Super bowl—that’s my term for the championship game between the two leagues&#8221; (Kansas City Star, July 17, 1966). The “b” of “bowl” was capitalized the next day in an Associated Press article that appeared in dozens of other newspapers.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Hunt suggested later that he may have thought of the name because his two children, Lamar Jr., then 10, and Sharon, 8, were playing all the time with a bouncy toy called the Super Ball.</p>
<p>The term had shown up a few years earlier in reference to a bowling championship: “What would they call the new Bowl game? The Super Bowl?” (Corona [CA] Daily Independent, Oct. 25, 1956). The term “bowl” here meant &#8220;place for bowling” or “event involving bowling,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p>The <em>OED</em> says the football usage ultimately comes from the use of “bowl” to mean &#8220;an oval or bowl-shaped stadium intended or used primarily for college football; (later) a stadium known as a venue for bowl games.”</p>
<p>The dictionary’s earliest example, which we’ve expanded here, cites remarks by Arthur Twining Hadley, president of Yale University, about the naming of the Yale Bowl, the college football stadium in New Haven, CT:</p>
<p>“I am glad that Yale, in spite of its classical traditions, prefers the good old word ‘bowl,’ with its savor of manly English sport, to the ‘coliseum’ of the Romans or the ‘stadium’ of the Greeks” (Yale Alumni Weekly, July 4, 1913).</p>
<p>The <em>OED</em> suggests that “Super Bowl” was specifically influenced by “Rose Bowl” and “similar names of college championship games,” and cites this reference to the first football “bowl game,” played at the Rose Bowl Stadium in Pasadena, CA:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Cougars inaugurated bowl game by beating Brown” (Salt Lake Tribune, Jan. 1, 1931).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, let’s take a look at the origins of the word “bowl.” The noun, spelled <em>bolen</em> in Old English, is derived from <em>bullǭ</em>, a prehistoric Germanic root for a round object. The earliest <em>OED</em> citation is from <em>Bald&#8217;s Leechbook</em>, an Anglo-Saxon medical text written around the mid-ninth century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">This passage, which we’ve expanded by restoring an ellipsis, describes a treatment for toothache, which in ancient times was believed to be caused by parasitic tooth worms:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Wið toþ wærce, gif wyrm ete, genim eald holen leaf &amp; heorot crop neoþeweardne &amp; saluian ufewearde, bewyl twy dæl on wætre, geot on bollan &amp; geona ymb; þonne feallað þa wyrmas on þone bollan.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">(“For tooth ache, if worm eats [a tooth], take an old holly leaf &amp; the lower part of heorot crop [perhaps hartwort] &amp; the upper part of sage, boil two portions in water, pour into a bowl, and open [the mouth] over it; then the worms will fall into the bowl.”)</p>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>[Note: In observation of Christmas week, we’re republishing a post that originally appeared on Dec. 23, 2022.] Q: Which is the more traditional version of this Christmas carol: “God Rest Ye, Merry Gentlemen” or “God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen”? I see it both ways, but the one with &#8220;you&#8221; looks better to me. A: You’re [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2025/12/god-rest-you-merry-gentlemen.html">Gentlemen, God rest you merry!</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>[Note: In observation of Christmas week, we’re republishing a post that originally appeared on Dec. 23, 2022.]</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: Which is the more traditional version of this Christmas carol: “God Rest Ye, Merry Gentlemen” or “God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen”? I see it both ways, but the one with &#8220;you&#8221; looks better to me.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: You’re right—“you” makes more sense than “ye” in this case, as we’ll explain later. In fact, the original pronoun in that early 18th-century carol was “you.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But that isn’t the only misunderstanding associated with the song. There&#8217;s that wayward comma too. Here’s the story.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Beginning in the Middle Ages, English speakers used “rest you” or “rest thee” with a positive adjective (“merry,” “well,” “tranquil,” “happy,” “content”) to mean “remain in that condition.” (The verb “rest” is used in a somewhat similar sense today in the expressions “rest assured” and “rest easy.”)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the earliest and most common of such expressions, the adjective was “merry,” according to citations in the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>. And at the time, “merry” had a meaning (happy, content, pleased) that’s now obsolete.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So in medieval English, the friendly salutation “rest you (or thee) merry” meant remain happy, content, or pleased. The <em>OED</em> explains it more broadly as “an expression of good wishes” that meant “peace and happiness to you.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The form “rest you merry” was used in addressing two or more people, while “rest thee merry” was used for just one. This is because our modern word “you,” the second-person pronoun, originally had four principal forms: the subjects were “ye” (plural) and “thou” (singular); the objects were “you” (plural) and “thee” (singular). The expression we’re discussing required an object pronoun.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>’s earliest example of the expression, in 13th-century Middle English, shows a single person being addressed: “Rest þe [thee] murie, sire Daris” (the letter þ, a thorn, represented a “th” sound). From <em>Floris and Blanchefleur</em> (circa 1250), a popular romantic tale that dates from the 1100s in Old French.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As early as the mid-1200s, according to <em>OED</em> citations, “you” began to replace the other second-person pronouns. By the early 1500s, “you” was serving all four purposes in ordinary usage: objective and nominative, singular and plural.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As a result, the usual form of the old expression became “rest you merry” even when only one person was addressed. And it was often preceded by “God” as a polite salutation, with the meaning “may God grant you peace and happiness,” the <em>OED</em> says. The dictionary cites several early examples of the formula:</p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“o louynge [loving] frende god rest you mery.” From an instructional book, <em>Floures for Latine Spekynge Gathered Oute of Terence </em>(1534)<em>, </em>by Nicholas Udall. (The English is presented as a translation of the Latin greeting <em>Amice salue</em>.)</li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“God rest you mery bothe and God be your guide.” From <em>Like Wil to Like </em>(1568), a morality play by Ulpian Fulwell.</li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;">“God rest you merry sir.” From Shakespeare’s <em>As You Like It </em>(c. 1600).</li>
</ul>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Soon after Shakespeare’s time, we find the formulaic “rest you merry” addressed to “gentlemen.” In plays of the 17th century in particular, it’s often spoken by a character in greeting or parting from friends.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The popular playwright John Fletcher, for example, used “rest you merry gentlemen” in at least two of his comedies: <em>Wit Without Money </em>(c. 1614) and <em>Monsieur Thomas</em> (c. 1610-16).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">It also appears in several other comedies of the period, including works by the pseudonymous “J. D., Gent” (<em>The Knave in Graine</em>, 1640), Abraham Cowley (<em>Cutter of Coleman-Street</em>, 1658), Thomas Southland (<em>Love a la Mode</em>, 1663), and William Mountfort (<em>Greenwich-Park</em>, 1691).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In most of the 17th-century examples we’ve found, there’s no comma in “God rest you merry gentlemen.” When a comma does appear, it comes after “merry,” not before: “Rest you merry, gentlemen.”  This is because “rest you merry” is addressed to the “gentlemen.&#8221;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In his comedy <em>Changes: or, Love in a Maze</em> (1632), James Shirley has “Gentlemen, rest you merry,” a use that more clearly illustrates the sense of the expression and removes any ambiguity.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">This brings us to the Christmas song “God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen”— the title as given in <em>The Oxford Book of Carols</em> and other authoritative collections. The oldest existing printed version of the song was published around 1700, though the lyrics were probably known orally before that.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As the <em>OED</em> says, “rest you merry” is no longer used as an English expression; it survives only in the carol. But the syntax of the title, the dictionary adds, “is frequently misinterpreted, <em>merry</em> being understood as an adjective qualifying <em>gentlemen</em>.” So the comma is often misplaced after “you,” as if those addressed were “merry gentlemen.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, the carol originally had no title. The words first appeared, as far as we can tell, in a single-page broadsheet entitled <em>Four Choice Carols for Christmas Holidays </em>with only a generic designation—“Carol  I. On Christmas-Day.” The broadsheet had no music, either; the words were sung to a variety of tunes.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The sheet was probably published in 1700 or 1701, according to the database Early English Books Online. Some commentators have said the lyrics existed earlier, but we haven’t found any documents to show this. The other three songs on the sheet are designated “Carol II. On St. Stephen&#8217;s-Day,” “Carol III. On St. John&#8217;s-Day,” and “Carol IV. On Innocent&#8217;s-Day.”  Here’s a <a href="https://ebba.english.ucsb.edu/ballad/31130/image#">facsimile</a> of the front side, with “Carol I” at left.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><a href="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/God-Rest-you-carol.jpeg"><img decoding="async" class="alignnone wp-image-31217" src="http://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/God-Rest-you-carol-186x300.jpeg" alt="" width="184" height="296" srcset="https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/God-Rest-you-carol-186x300.jpeg 186w, https://grammarphobia.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/God-Rest-you-carol.jpeg 253w" sizes="(max-width: 184px) 100vw, 184px" /></a></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“God rest you merry Gentlemen” (without a comma) is the first line of “Carol I,” and it later became used as the title. It appeared as the title in some printings of the carol by the late 1700s.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But well into the 19th century the song was sometimes referred to simply as “Old Christmas Carol” (in <em>Sam Weller</em>, a play by William Thomas Moncreiff, London, 1837) or “A Christmas Carol” (in The Baltimore County Union, a weekly newspaper in Towsontown, MD, Dec. 23, 1865).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">For the most part, music publishers over the years have printed the title with “you” (not “ye”) and with the comma after “merry,” a form that accurately represents the original meaning. But in books, newspapers, and other writing the title has also appeared with “ye,” a misplaced comma, or both.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Why the misplaced comma? Apparently the old senses of “rest” and “merry” were forgotten, and the title was reinterpreted in ordinary usage. It was understood to mean that a group of “merry gentlemen” were encouraged to relax and be jolly.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>OED</em>’s earliest example of the misconception dates from the early 19th century, where Samuel Jackson Pratt refers to “God Rest You, Merry Gentlemen” as “a time-embrowned ditty” (<em>Gleanings in England</em>, 2nd ed., 1803).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And why the shift from “you” to “ye”?  Our guess is that it represents an attempt to make the carol sound older or more “traditional.” (Not coincidentally, “ye” began appearing in place of “you” in 18th- and 19th-century reprints of those old comedies we mentioned above, as if to make them more antique.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">We’ve found scores of “ye” versions of the carol dating from the 1840s onwards in ordinary British and American usage.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A search of Google’s <a href="https://books.google.com/ngrams/graph?content=God+Rest+You+Merry+Gentlemen,God+Rest+Ye+Merry+Gentlemen&amp;year_start=1800&amp;year_end=2009&amp;corpus=0&amp;smoothing=3">Ngram viewer</a> shows that “you” versions were predominant in books and journals until the mid-20th century. But in the 1960s, “ye” versions began to rise, and by the ’80s they had surpassed the “you” versions. (Placement of the comma isn’t searchable on Ngram.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Today, both the “ye” and the misplaced comma are ubiquitous in common usage, despite the way the title is printed by most music publishers and academic presses.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Perhaps the music of the carol bears some of the blame for the wayward comma. While the song has had several different musical settings, it’s now sung to music, most likely imported from Europe, that some scholars believe was first published in Britain in 1796. And the tune doesn’t allow for a pause before “gentlemen,” so the ear doesn’t sense a comma there.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As the music scholar Edward Wickham writes, “The comprehension of whole sentences of text, when sung, relies in part on the perception of how those sentences are segmented and organised.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“The music to the Christmas carol &#8216;God rest you merry, Gentlemen,’ ” Wickham says, “makes no provision for the comma and thus is routinely misunderstood as &#8216;God rest you, merry Gentlemen.’ ” (“Tales from Babel: Musical Adventures in the Science of Hearing,” a chapter in <em>Experimental Affinities in Music</em>, 2015, edited by Paulo de Assis.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">One final observation. All this reminds us of an entirely different “ye” misunderstanding—the mistaken use of “ye” as an article. This misconception shows up in signage of the “Ye Olde Gift Shoppe” variety, an attempt at quaintness that we wrote about in <a href="http://grammarphobia.com/blog/2009/12/ye-olde-knickknack-shoppe.html">2009</a> and again in <a href="http://grammarphobia.com/blog/2016/06/shoppe.html">2016</a>.</p>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>Q: I thought I might further muddy the waters of the wonderful word featured in your post about “dasn’t.” I once saw it defined as a contraction of “darest not,” but frustratingly I can’t recall the source. I first saw “dasn’t” as a youngster reading the Huck Finn novel you mention. A: In “I dasn’t [&#8230;]</p>
The post <a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2025/12/dasnt.html">We dasn’t commit ourselves</a> first appeared on <a href="https://grammarphobia.com">Grammarphobia</a>.]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I thought I might further muddy the waters of the wonderful word featured in your post about “<a href="https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2009/06/i-dasnt-scratch.html">dasn’t</a>.” I once saw it defined as a contraction of “darest not,” but frustratingly I can’t recall the source. I first saw “dasn’t” as a youngster reading the Huck Finn novel you mention.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: In “I dasn’t scratch,” our 2009 post  (updated in 2022), we include “dasn’t” among the many dialectal contractions of “dare not.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As we say in that post, the negative “dare” contractions in the <em>Dictionary of Regional American English</em> include “daren’t,” “durn’t,” “dursent,” “durstn’t,” “ders(e)n’t,” “daredn’t,” “dar(e)sn’t,” “darshin,” “das(s)n’t,” “das(s)ent,” and “dazzent.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Similarly, <em>Merriam-Webster</em> online describes “dasn’t,” “dass’nt,” and less commonly “dassent” as dialectal versions of “dare not.&#8221;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">However, the <em>DARE</em> and <em>Merriam-Webster</em> entries raise this question: How did “daren’t,” the most obvious (and standard) contraction of “dare not,” end up as the dialectal shortening “dasn’t”?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A “Word History” item in <em>Merriam-Webster</em> answers by describing “dasn’t” as “partly contraction of (<em>thou</em>) <em>darst not</em>&#8221; (from Middle English), partly contraction of (<em>he</em>) <em>dares not</em>.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for your suggestion, we haven’t seen any authoritative source that describes “dasn’t” as a contraction of “darest not.” But we’ve found some plausible comments from readers of reputable websites suggesting that “darest not” might have become “dasn’t” through the loss of the “r” sound before “s” by assimilation, as in “cuss” for “curse”  and “bust” for “burst.&#8221;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The linguist Anatoly Liberman discusses this loss of the “r” sound in “Do you ‘cuss’ your stars when you go ‘bust’?” a 2012 post on the <a href="https://blog.oup.com/2012/09/word-origin-cuss-curse-bust-burst-etymology/">OUPblog</a> of Oxford University Press. Liberman doesn’t mention “dasn’t,” but a comment by a reader, John Cowan, cites the contraction of “darest not” to “dasn’t” as an example of “r”-loss:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Some other examples are <em>passel</em> ‘large amount’ &lt; <em>parcel</em>, <em>gal</em> &gt; <em>girl</em>, <em>palsy</em> ultimately from <em>paralysis</em>, and many more that are archaic, like <em>skasely</em> &lt; <em>scarcely</em>, <em>hoss</em> &lt; <em>horse</em>, <em>podner</em> &lt; <em>partner</em>, and <em>dasn’t</em> &lt; <em>darest not</em>.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in “Old-timey contractions,” a 2022 post by the linguist Mark Liberman on the <a href="https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=55855">Language Log</a>, reader John Swindle, commenting on possible “r” loss examples, suggests “dasn’t” could be “a contraction of ‘darest not,’ ” and not limited to “singular or even to present tense.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">What do we think? Well, it’s not impossible that “dasn’t” evolved as a contraction of “darest not.” But until we see some solid evidence, we dasn’t commit ourselves.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I was catching up with <em>The Wire</em>, the TV crime series. In episode one of season five, originally aired in 2008, editors at The Baltimore Sun tell a reporter that a building is evacuated, not a person, except when given an enema. I looked in a number of dictionaries and they disagree. Where did this myth come from?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: We had a similar experience in the early 1980s when we were working at The New York Times. An editor there also insisted that you evacuate (or empty) a building, not a person—unless the person is getting an enema.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Interestingly, the principal writer on <em>The Wire</em>, David Simon, had been a crime reporter for The Sun, and may have run into an editor or two like the one we encountered at The Times.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">There’s no legitimate reason to restrict the verb “evacuate” to the narrow definition advocated by that Times editor and by the fictional editors on <em>The Wire</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><em>The New York Times Manual of Style and Usage</em> didn’t have an entry for “evacuate” in the 1980s, and it still doesn&#8217;t. The same is true of <em>The Associated Press Stylebook</em>, the guide generally followed by The Baltimore Sun—the newspaper depicted on <em>The Wire</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And the house dictionary at the two newspapers, <em>Webster’s New World College Dictionary</em>, had a much more expansive view of “evacuate” in the 1980s and still does.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The definition in the dictionary’s second edition (1979) includes three senses that are transitive (used with an object) and two that are intransitive (without an object). All five were then—and are today—standard English:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Transitive: “1. To make empty; remove the contents of; specif., to remove air from so as to make a vacuum. 2. To discharge bodily waste, esp. feces. 3. To remove (inhabitants, troops, etc.) from (a place or area), as for protective or strategic purposes; withdraw from.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Intransitive: “1. To withdraw, as from a besieged town or area of danger. 2. To discharge bodily waste, esp. feces.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">(We have the second, third, fourth, and current fifth editions of <em>Webster’s New World</em> in our library, as well as various editions of many other standard dictionaries.)</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Why did the real and fictional editors take such a restrictive view of “evacuate”? Our guess is that they mistakenly believed the first and second senses listed in <em>Webster’s New World</em> (transitive #1 and #2) were the only legitimate meanings. Or perhaps they were overly influenced by the etymological roots of the verb.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>, an etymological reference, says “evacuate” ultimately comes from the classical Latin <em>evacuare</em>, which Pliny the Elder used “with the sense of to empty (the bowels)” in <em>Historia Naturalis</em>, an encyclopedic collection of scientific knowledge in ancient times.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When the verb entered English in the 16th century, the <em>OED</em> says, it meant “to empty, clear out the contents of (a vessel or receptacle).” In the dictionary’s earliest citation, it’s used in the sense of to empty the bowels:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“After you haue euacuated your body, &amp; trussed your poyntes, kayme your heade oft” (“After you have evacuated your bowels and tied your laces, comb your hair often”). From <em>A Compendyous Regyment or a Dyetary of Helth</em> (1542), a guide to health and hygiene by the physician and author Andrew Borde.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the 17th century, the verb took on the transitive and intransitive senses of evacuating people from a place. The first transitive citation in the <em>OED</em> refers to people evacuated during William the Conqueror’s invasion of England:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“Action had pretty well evacuated the idle people, which are the stock of rapine.” From “Short History of William I,” written by Sir Henry Wotton sometime before 1639 and published in <em>Collectanea Curiosa</em> (1781), edited by John Gutch.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The first intransitive example, which we’ve expanded, is from <em>A Discourse About Trade</em> (1690), by the English economist and merchant Josiah Child. This passage refers to people who evacuate, or leave, England to work in the West Indies overseeing slaves:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;The People that evacuate from us to Barbadoes, and the other West. India Plantations, as was before hinted, do commonly work one English man to ten or eight Blacks.”</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p style="font-weight: 400;">Q: I’ve been noticing lately the strange use of “went to go” to form the past tense, as in “went to go see a movie,” “went to go swim,” and “went to go download a video.” I see this as an example of a lack of awareness of how English works.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">A: We’d describe the use of “went to go” in your examples as colloquial or informal rather than redundant.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The construction is extremely common in speech, in quoted material, and in casual posts on TikTok and Reddit. But it&#8217;s rarely found in edited prose.</p>
<p>In fact, &#8220;went to go&#8221; often describes a situation in which an action was attempted but failed. That use of the expression has been around for quite some time.</p>
<p>Here’s an example from an article in The New York Times of June 25, 1865—yes, 1865!—about a trial in which a woman accuses a ship’s captain of raping her:</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The Times said the woman testified that “being very sleepy, she went to go to bed and could not find the key to her door.” The captain eventually let her in, she said, and he later assaulted her.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, the verb “go” doesn’t mean only to move, travel, or proceed somewhere. It has many other senses, so it’s not necessarily redundant to use the verb twice in the same sentence. For example, “go” used progressively can express the future, as in “I&#8217;m going to go to the movies.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And in an expression like “go see a movie,” the verb “go” appears “often with the sense of motion weakened or absent,” according to the <em>Oxford English Dictionary</em>.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The “go” here is in its base form (infinitive, imperative, subjunctive, etc.), the <em>OED</em> says, “with a following verb also in the base form.” Examples: “go look,” “go find,” “go get,” and so on.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">The dictionary has examples for the usage dating back to Anglo-Saxon days, though it says this use of “go” is now colloquial or informal in American English and nonstandard in British English.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">As for the use of “went to go + infinitive,” the exact phrase you’re noticing (&#8220;went to go find,&#8221; &#8220;went to go visit,&#8221; &#8220;went to go buy,&#8221; etc.), it appears to have increased noticeably in the late 20th and early 21st century.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s an example from Monica Lewinsky&#8217;s testimony before a federal grand jury in Washington about her relationship with President Clinton (Aug. 20, 1998).</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Asked about her notorious blue dress, she remarked: “I didn&#8217;t really realize that there was anything on it until I went to go wear it again and I had gained too much weight that I couldn&#8217;t fit into it.”</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">And here’s a more recent example from a Nov. 3, 2025, article in The New York Times about the artist Greer Lankton, who died of a cocaine overdose in 1996 at the age of 38.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">“I went to go see her in Chicago three months before she died. I think she was desperate to die; that’s all she could talk about” (the jewelry designer Paul Monroe on visiting Lankton, known for her lifelike hand-sewn dolls).</p>
