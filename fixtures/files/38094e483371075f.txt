Today, <em>wraith</em> may refer broadly to ghost or specter, or figuratively to something regarded as ghostly or insubstantial, as when <em>New Yorker</em> film critic Anthony Lane referred to an actor's "wraith of a mustache."</p>

In fact, <em>reticent</em> took on its “reluctant” sense a mere 50 years after first appearing in English in the early 19th century with the meaning “inclined to be silent or uncommunicative.” Though some brows may furrow, the development of <em>reticent</em>’s newer meaning has some logic to it: English speakers first used <em>reticent</em> synonymously with <em>reluctant</em> when the context was speech, as in “he was reticent to talk about his past,” keeping the word close to its “silent” beginnings. Eventually, however, exclusive association with speech was abandoned, and one can now be reticent to do anything, even if it’s to admit that language is not immutable.</p>
